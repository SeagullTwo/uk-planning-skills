# Committee speech

Write the speech you will actually stand up and deliver at a planning committee.

Somebody has a slot. It is two, three or four minutes, it happens once, and it is heard by
members who may not have read the papers closely. This skill writes what they say.

## What makes a speech different from an objection

**The objection is read at leisure by the case officer, who is paid to read it. The speech is
heard once, aloud, under a clock, by people deciding a dozen items in an evening.**

So the job is not to compress the objection. It is to **select** the few points that can move a
vote, **frame** each as the decision members are actually taking, and **sequence** them across
the speakers without repetition.

## It argues one side, and says so

This skill writes **objector** speeches. It is written as the objector's advocate, and that is
declared rather than disguised — the rest of this repo is careful about neutrality, and a reader
is entitled to know where the posture changes.

It is legitimate for the same reason `policy-representation` is: an objector is a party, and
writing a party's case at their instruction is not an analysis skill quietly taking a side. What
would not be legitimate is pretending the output is balanced.

**The integrity constraint survives the advocacy, and it is where the value is:**

- **It will not manufacture a ground.** A point the papers do not support hands the officer a
  correction to make in the reply, in front of the members you were trying to persuade.
- **It will tell you when the honest ask is not refusal** — when the realistic outcome is
  deferral or tighter conditions — rather than writing a demand the committee cannot grant.
- **It concedes what is settled**, early, because that is what buys the right to be believed on
  everything else.

Speaking in support? The mechanics all transfer — the clock, the register, the allocation, the
ask at the end. The stage gateways assume you are arguing against, so use them as scaffolding
and say that is what you are doing.

## Five things it insists on

- **Write to the clock.** 130–150 words a minute for a nervous person reading aloud, converted
  to a word budget, with the count stated so the speaker can practise against a timer. A speaker
  who overruns is cut off mid-sentence, often before the ask.

- **The ask goes in the final two sentences.** It is the one place that is always reached. If
  time is cut, everything else goes before it does.

- **Respect the room; do not lecture it.** Being told their own law and duties — *"you must"*,
  statute quoted back, appeal costs as a warning — reads as preaching and puts backs up, which
  is the last thing an objector can afford. Make the point as help, not instruction, and let the
  council's own advisers carry the argument.

- **Concede what is settled.** One overstated point, spoken aloud and unchallenged, discredits
  everything around it — and members remember it.

- **Never let a later speaker claim an earlier speaker's point with "I".** Three different
  people are speaking. It is an easy tell that a slate was ghost-written.

## Where the material comes from

**A speech is the last thing to write, not the first.** Compose it from the written case; do not
re-derive it, or the speech will drift from the objection on file and the officer will say so in
the reply.

| You need | Run |
|---|---|
| The strongest grounds, already anchored to policy | `committee-pack-review` |
| Which considerations are engaged at all | `application-triage` |
| The development plan and how the scheme scores | `policy-compliance-assessment` |
| The written objection this must not contradict | `policy-representation` |
| Whether refusal is the realistic ask at all | `planning-balance` |
| Current framework wording — **verify before citing** | `national-planning-policy` |

`committee-pack-review`'s *"if you are minded to refuse"* case is precisely this skill's input:
the strongest grounds, each carrying its policy and whether it is evidenced or asserted.
