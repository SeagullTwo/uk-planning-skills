# Noise representation on a planning application

Help a member of the public produce a rigorous, credible representation on the **noise and
disturbance** merits of a planning application — the kind a case officer and an
environmental health officer can act on and lift into the officer report. The skill does
three things:

1. **Evaluate** the applicant's noise evidence against current policy and the applicable
   standards, and identify the material deficiencies.
2. **Map** each deficiency to the specific national and local policy, standard or guidance
   it engages.
3. **Draft** the representation in clear, precise, concise language — or advise that the
   evidence is sound and no sustainable objection exists.

## When to use

The user has a planning application and wants to object (or find out whether they *can*
object) on noise grounds. Trigger phrases: "object on noise grounds", "is this Noise Impact
Assessment any good", "critique this acoustic report / BS 4142 assessment", "they want to
open the beer garden until 11", "extraction plant right behind my garden", "they're building
flats next to our workshop and we'll get the complaints", "the noise survey was done in the
wrong place".

Not this skill: ecology, heritage, flooding, transport capacity — separate matters. Air
quality, light and odour are neighbouring limbs of the same NPPF policy (P3) and the method
here transfers, but the standards catalogue is noise-specific.

## The three directions — establish which one you are in before anything else

Almost every mistake in a noise representation comes from arguing the wrong direction.

- **Direction A — the development is the noise *source*.** A new or intensified
  noise-generating use next to existing noise-sensitive receptors: a licensed premises and
  its outdoor drinking, dining, smoking or terrace areas; extraction, refrigeration or
  air-handling plant; deliveries, servicing and waste collection; an extension of opening or
  delivery hours; an intensified sports, leisure or industrial use. The tests are **NPPF
  P3(2)(b)** (no unacceptable noise *on or beyond the site*) and **P3(2)(d)** (mitigate and
  reduce adverse impacts to a minimum; no exposure at a **significant observed adverse
  effect** level; maintain the character of tranquil areas).
- **Direction B — the development is the *receptor*.** New noise-sensitive development —
  housing, student accommodation, care homes, schools — proposed in a noisy location, near
  a road, railway, industrial estate, sports ground, music venue or pub. The tests are
  **P3(2)(a)** (healthy living conditions for the occupiers, avoiding exposure to noise with
  an unacceptable adverse effect on health and quality of life) and **P4** (the
  agent-of-change principle).

- **Direction C — the development is *both*, internally.** The scheme generates the noise and
  receives it, within its own red line: a living room or kitchen over a neighbouring flat's
  bedroom, the scheme's own parking or bin store under its own habitable windows, a shared
  access path or stair past the only window of a studio flat, HMO circulation space. The test
  is **P3(2)(a)** — healthy living conditions for the occupiers — and, where the internal
  arrangement is the problem, the design policies. **P4 is not engaged and citing it is an
  error**: there is no existing activity and no agent of change, because the same development
  is on both sides of the relationship.

**Many schemes are more than one** — a mixed-use block with flats above a bar receives noise
from the bar (B), generates plant and servicing noise for its neighbours (A), and may put its
own refuse store beneath its own bedroom windows (C). Run every direction that applies and say
which points belong to which.

**Direction C is easy to miss and is regularly decisive.** It attracts no noise assessment at
all, because assessments are commissioned to answer an external question — so the usual finding
is that the *layout* has never been assessed for internal noise transfer and nothing before the
authority shows it would be acceptable. Look at the stacking of rooms between units, what sits
under and over habitable rooms, and what every window actually faces.

**A warning about agent of change, because it cuts both ways.** P4 protects *existing*
activities from unreasonable restriction by new development approved after they were
established. If the user's concern is a new pub next to their home, P4 is not their friend —
the *pub* is the newcomer and P3 does the work. If the user's concern is new housing that
will generate complaints against a long-standing business (theirs, or one they value), P4 is
the strongest policy available and P4(3) is the sharp edge: **both lawful current *and
permitted* levels of operation** of the existing activity must be taken into account, not
just what it happens to do today. Do not cite P4 in a Direction A objection; it will read as
though the skill does not understand the policy.

## Check the route before you check the policy — prior approval is not a planning application

**Before citing any policy, establish whether this is a planning application or a prior
approval under the General Permitted Development Order.** Conversions to residential are
frequently the latter — Class MA (commercial to dwellinghouses), Class Q (agricultural
buildings to dwellinghouses), Class G (upward extension over commercial) — and on that route
almost everything below changes:

- **The development plan does not apply.** Section 70 of the 1990 Act is not engaged on a
  prior approval application, so the local plan's noise and amenity policies are not the test
  and **s.38(6) does not operate**. An objection built on them is aimed at the wrong target.
  The Framework comes in only so far as paragraph **W(10)(b)** brings it.
- **The noise test is the one in the Class itself, and it is narrower.** Read the Class. For
  example, Class MA asks about "the impacts of noise from commercial premises on the intended
  occupiers"; Class Q asks separately about the noise impacts *of the development* and about
  whether the location or siting makes the development "otherwise impractical or undesirable"
  — which the PPG glosses as, respectively, "not sensible or realistic" and "harmful or
  objectionable". Receptor-side noise usually lands under the "impractical or undesirable"
  limb, not the noise limb, and saying so correctly is worth more than any amount of policy
  citation.
- **Insufficient information is itself a ground.** Paragraph **W** permits refusal where the
  applicant has not provided sufficient information to determine whether the prior approval
  conditions are met — which is why the absence of a noise assessment bites particularly hard
  on this route.
- **The scope of what can be objected to is limited** to the matters the Class reserves for
  prior approval. Objections outside that scope are not merely weak; they are inadmissible.

Get the Class and its sub-paragraph numbers from the current GPDO text at run time, and verify
them — the classes and their conditions have been amended repeatedly. Where the application is
a prior approval, say so in the representation and frame every point against the Class test.

## The integrity principle and three framing rules (read before drafting)

**Only object where the evidence is genuinely inadequate or the impact genuinely
unacceptable.** If the survey is at the right receptor, over a representative period, the
right standard is applied in full, maximum levels and character are addressed, the
assumptions are justified and the mitigation is specified and conditionable, there is **no
sustainable objection** — say so. Don't manufacture points from a typo when the substance is
sound; treat "don't object" as a valid output.

Three rules specific to noise:

- **Attack the method, not the decibel.** "It will be too loud" is an assertion a decision-
  maker cannot act on, and an objector has no measurements to back it. The defensible
  ground is almost always that the **assessment does not answer the question the policy
  asks** — wrong receptor, unrepresentative baseline, averaged away, character never
  assessed, worst case never modelled, mitigation assumed perfect. That is a finding a case
  officer can adopt and an inspector can uphold.
- **Do not argue "the other regime will sort it out" — and expect the applicant to.**
  **P3(3)** and **DM7(1)** now direct the decision-maker to assume, *unless there is clear
  evidence to the contrary*, that separate regimes (statutory nuisance, environmental
  permitting, licensing) will operate effectively. That assumption is rebuttable but you
  must actually rebut it with evidence — a complaint history, an unresolved enforcement
  record, a consultee saying the controls have not worked — not with the assertion that
  enforcement is slow. Equally, the applicant cannot use the assumption to avoid the
  land-use question: whether the *use of land* is acceptable is for the planning system, and
  DM7(2) only bars *duplicating* other regimes' controls.
- **Watch the ratchet.** Noise-generating uses are frequently expanded an increment at a
  time — a temporary permission made permanent, a capacity increase, an hour here, a
  variation of a condition there. On a **s.73 variation, s.96A amendment or reserved-matters**
  application, the question is what this change, *and the accumulated changes*, do to the
  protection the original permission secured. Rebuild the chain first — the
  **application-triage** skill's `references/amendment-applications.md` has the method — and
  do not reopen what the parent permission settled.

**Classify every point's ask — (A) refuse, (B) don't determine yet, or (C) condition it.**
An evidential deficiency is not itself a reason for refusal. For each confirmed point be
explicit about which outcome it supports:

- **(A)** the evidence *demonstrates* an unacceptable effect or clear policy conflict → a
  refusal reason;
- **(B)** the evidence is *insufficient* for the Council to reach the conclusion P3 requires
  (no maximum levels, no character assessment, unrepresentative baseline) → the application
  should **not be determined** until the information is provided;
- **(C)** the issue can be adequately controlled → ask for the *specific* condition (hours,
  capacity cap, a boundary noise limit, an acoustic specification, delivery and servicing
  hours, a management plan secured before first use).

Most deficiency findings are **(B)**, not (A) — claiming (A) on (B) evidence is the classic
credibility mistake. And test every point against (C): if a precise, enforceable condition
would lawfully and satisfactorily resolve it, ask for that rather than refusal. But apply
(C) honestly — see D3 in the catalogue: a condition that depends on how a *particular
operator* chooses to behave is not a control on the land, and a condition must be precise
enough to be enforced (**DM6(1)(c)**).

**(B) is not the soft option the label suggests, and its force depends on the stage.** At
application stage (B) means what it says: the authority should not determine the application
until the information is there. **At appeal, an unremedied (B) is decisive** — inspectors do
not adjourn for better evidence, they dismiss, because the burden of demonstrating
acceptability sits on the appellant and an evidential gap is a failure to discharge it. So a
(B) finding is not a lesser version of (A); it is the same deficiency at an earlier stage,
and it should be pressed with that consequence made explicit. Say, in terms, that the
authority cannot make the P3 judgement on the material before it — not that it would be
preferable to have more information.

## What you need first

- The **application reference and council** (and, ideally, the documents).
- The **noise documents themselves** — you cannot critique what you have not read. Uploaded,
  pasted or already-downloaded files work directly; the companion **planning-document-search**
  skill is only needed when you don't have them. Prioritise:
  - the **Noise Impact Assessment / acoustic report**, with its appendices — the raw survey
    data, calibration and measurement records, model inputs and contour plots are where the
    findings are, not the executive summary;
  - the **plant schedule and elevations**, the **layout and boundary-treatment drawings**
    (capacity, seating, barrier heights and materials), and the **servicing and delivery**
    details;
  - the **transport statement** and the **planning statement** — not for their own sake, but
    because they state the delivery numbers, servicing arrangements, capacity and hours that
    the acoustic report has assumed, and are where contradictions with it surface (**E6**);
  - any **management plan**, **premises management plan** or **noise management plan** relied
    on;
  - the **environmental health consultation response** — often decisive; read it first;
  - the **application form's** hours, capacity and covers figures, and the **proposed
    conditions**;
  - on amendments, the **parent permission's noise conditions**, its officer report and every
    earlier amendment.
- The **local plan's** noise, amenity and pollution policies, and any **local technical
  guidance** on noise and planning (many authorities publish one, with local criteria that
  are more specific than national policy — cite it where it exists).
- For Direction B, what the **existing activity** is lawfully and *permittedly* allowed to do
  (P4(3)) — its permission, conditions, licence and operating pattern.

## Workflow

### Step 1 — Intake, direction and read
Gather the documents. Identify: the proposal and its **stage** (full / outline / reserved
matters / s.73 / s.96A); **which direction** (A, B or both); the noise **sources** and the
**receptors**, including upper floors, gardens, balconies and any non-residential sensitive
receptors (schools, care homes, hospitals); the **hours** applied for; and whether
**environmental health** has objected, said "no objection", or asked for more information.
Read the environmental health response first — it is the closest thing to an ally the
representation has, and where it has already made a point, your job is to reinforce and
evidence it, not to restate it in weaker terms.

### Step 2 — Evaluate against the deficiency catalogue (function 1)
Work through [`references/deficiency-catalogue.md`](references/deficiency-catalogue.md)
against the documents. For each candidate deficiency, confirm it is **actually present** and
**material**, and capture the *specific* evidence — the document, author, date, and the
paragraph, table, figure or appendix. Quote the assessment's own words, and note where its
stated methodology promises something its body never delivers. Grade findings
(decision-critical vs minor) and lead with the decision-critical. Drop anything you cannot
evidence.

The cross-cutting tests, in rough order of how often they decide the point:

- Do the acoustic report's **assumed facts match the applicant's other documents** — the
  transport statement's delivery numbers, the drawings' servicing points and capacity, the
  application form's hours? Read the report against the rest of the submission before you read
  it against the standards: a contradiction between two of the applicant's own documents needs
  no acoustics to find and goes to whether the Council has a coherent description of the
  development at all (**E6**).
- Is the **character** of the noise assessed — tonality, impulsivity, intermittency,
  distinguishability, human voice — or only its level?
- Are **maximum levels** (LAmax) assessed, or only averages? Is the averaging period long
  enough to hide the event that matters?
- Is the standard or guidance being applied **designed for this kind of source**, and is it
  applied **in full** rather than for one convenient number? Does the **authority's own**
  published noise guidance set a criterion the assessment has ignored — or supply figures the
  assessment has borrowed out of the context they were derived for (**B12**)?
- Are the **baseline** measurements at a location, over a period and in conditions that
  represent the receptor — and do they exclude the activity being assessed?
- Are the **nearest and most exposed** receptors the ones assessed?
- Are the **occupancy, worst-case and hours** assumptions stated and justified?
- Is **mitigation** specified, deliverable, and secured — or assumed?
- Does the assessment identify the **LOAEL and SOAEL** it is applying, and demonstrate the
  P3(2)(d) test is met?

### Step 3 — Map to policy and standards (function 2)
For every confirmed deficiency, attach the precise instrument from
[`references/national-guidance.md`](references/national-guidance.md) — NPPF **P3**, **P4**,
**DM6**, **DM7**, **DM2** and Annex C; the PPG on noise and its effect-level hierarchy; the
Noise Policy Statement for England; and the applicable standards (BS 4142, BS 8233, ProPG,
the AVO Guide, the IEMA guidelines, the WHO guidance) — plus the **local plan** policies and
any local technical guidance. Cite specifically, never "best practice" in the abstract, and
verify any citation you are unsure of rather than guessing. Note that the PPG noise pages
pre-date the coded Framework and still refer to superseded paragraph numbers — cite the
guidance for its *substance* and the NPPF by its current codes.

### Step 4 — Draft (function 3)
Draft to [`references/house-style.md`](references/house-style.md) and
[`references/objection-template.md`](references/objection-template.md): header → RE line
(note the stage) → opening (consultation status; material considerations; on an amendment,
what you do not seek to reopen) → framework list → numbered conclusion-headed points (quoted
evidence, why it matters, the ask and its timing) → numbered **Summary of requests** →
optional "if minded to approve" conditions → objection sentence → sign-off. Keep it concise;
lead with the decision-critical points.

### Step 5 — Check before sending
- Every point is evidenced from the documents or cited guidance; nothing asserted.
- Quotes are accurate and referenced (§ / Table / Figure / Appendix).
- The **direction** is right, and P4 is not cited against the user's own interest.
- It reads as material considerations, not a complaint about the clientele. Objections to
  the *type of people* a use attracts are not material and will discredit the rest; the
  material question is noise and disturbance from the use of land.
- Every point is classified **(A) demonstrated harm / (B) insufficient evidence / (C)
  conditionable** — and no point asks for refusal where a precise, enforceable condition
  would lawfully and satisfactorily do.
- Any requested condition is precise enough to be enforced (DM6(1)(c)), does not depend on the
  identity or goodwill of the current operator, and actually reaches the source complained of —
  a plant-noise limit does not control patrons, and a condition on one outdoor area does not
  control people who can walk into another. Check it against
  [`references/model-conditions.md`](references/model-conditions.md), and make sure you have not
  asked for a deferred assessment, an "inaudible" standard or a tailpiece.
- Third-party material (neighbours' accounts, complaint records, consultee responses) is used
  minimally and fairly, and personal details of other people are not reproduced without need.
- Deadline: note if consultation has closed; representations remain material while the
  application is undecided.
- **Hand back to a human, with the two warnings:** the draft must be read and checked by a
  person before submission, and submitting it puts a **public document in the user's name**
  on the council's portal — confirm they're content with the content and the personal
  details included.

## Scope and limitations

- **Not legal advice, and no warranty.** This helps a lay objector marshal evidence and cite
  policy; it is not a substitute for a solicitor or an acoustician, guarantees no outcome,
  and is provided "as is" with no warranty of accuracy or fitness for purpose.
- **Not an acoustic assessment.** This skill critiques someone else's measurements and
  modelling. It does not produce noise predictions, and must not invent, estimate or
  "correct" decibel figures. Where a number is needed and none exists, that *absence* is the
  point, argued as such.
- **Human review is necessary before submitting.** A person must read the draft, check every
  quote and citation against the actual documents, and confirm the points are correct and
  fair for this application.
- **A UK planning representation is a public document in your name.** Comments are normally
  published on the council's planning portal, typically including the submitter's name (and
  sometimes address), and kept on the record. Tell the user before they submit and let them
  choose what personal details to include.
- **Planning only.** Licensing (a premises licence or its variation, under the Licensing Act
  2003) is a **separate regime** with different objectives, different parties, a short
  representation window and its own procedure. Noise disturbance can be relevant to both, but
  a planning representation is not a licensing representation and the tests are not the same.
  Tell the user when a parallel licensing application appears to exist and point them to the
  licensing authority's own process; do not draft the licensing representation here.
- **England-focused.** The NPPF and PPG cited are England; Wales, Scotland and Northern
  Ireland differ — flag it and adjust the citations when the application is outside England.
- **Evidence-bound.** Every point stands or falls on the submitted documents and the cited
  guidance.
- **The honest answer is sometimes "don't object."** Treat that as a valid, valuable output.
