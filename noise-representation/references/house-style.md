# House style — how a strong noise representation reads

The goal is a representation a busy case officer and an environmental health officer can act
on: **concise, specific, evidenced, and easy to lift into the officer report.** These are the
writing rules the skill applies at the drafting stage (function 3). They mirror the approach of
the other topic representation skills; the noise-specific moves are flagged.

## Voice and stance

- **Measured and expert, never emotive.** Noise objections fail more often on tone than on
  substance. The force comes from specificity — quoting the applicant's own assessment against
  itself — not from describing how bad it is to live there. One sentence of lived experience,
  placed precisely, carries more than a page of it.
- **Material considerations only.** Everything must be a planning material consideration: the
  adequacy of the noise evidence, the effect on living conditions, compliance with national and
  local policy and the applicable standards. **Not** the type of people the use attracts, not
  the operator's identity or corporate conduct, not competition, not property values. This is
  the discipline that separates a representation officers act on from one they discount.
- **Critique the document, not the author.** "The assessment does not address maximum levels",
  never "the consultant has deliberately concealed". Where you are alleging a pattern of
  method error, describe the method and let the pattern speak.
- **Concede what is sound; don't manufacture.** If the survey is properly located and the
  standard properly applied, say so and move to the point that isn't. If the whole thing is
  sound, advise *not* objecting.
- **Align with the consultee, don't ventriloquise them.** Where environmental health has raised
  a point, cite their response and add the evidence they may not have — do not restate their
  point in your own weaker words as though it were yours.

## Aim at the winnable tests

There is no "severe" threshold gate in noise policy of the kind transport has, and no
refusal-directing policy either: **P3 states an unacceptability test, not a refusal trigger**
(see `national-guidance.md` on S4(2)(c)). So the winnable pitch is rarely "this will be
unacceptably loud" — an objector has no measurements. It is:

- **the assessment does not answer the question P3 asks** — no maximum levels, no character
  assessment, no LOAEL/SOAEL, no worst case;
- **the baseline or the receptor is wrong**, so every later figure is wrong;
- **the methodology named is not the methodology followed**;
- **the mitigation is assumed rather than specified, or is management rather than control**;
  and
- **the prediction is contradicted by outcomes** where the same method has been used before.

Those are findings a case officer can adopt, an environmental health officer can endorse, and
an inspector can uphold.

## Structure

1. **Header block** — your name/address/email, the Council's address, date, case officer if
   known.
2. **RE line** — application reference + site + a one-line description (note whether full,
   outline, reserved matters, s73 variation or s96A amendment — this frames everything).
3. **Opening** — note consultation status; the matters are material considerations while the
   application is undecided; ask that the representation be placed on the file and copied to
   environmental health. On an amendment, state what you do *not* seek to reopen.
4. **What you address** — name the noise documents by author and date, so it is clear the
   critique is of the evidence actually submitted.
5. **Framework list** — a short bulleted list of the policy and standards engaged (see
   `national-guidance.md`). Signals rigour and anchors every later point.
6. **Numbered sections** — one deficiency per numbered sub-section, each with a **bold one-line
   heading that states the point as a conclusion**, then 1–3 tight paragraphs.
7. **Summary of requests** — a single numbered list of concrete asks, mostly ending "before
   determination".
8. **"If minded to approve"** (optional but valuable in noise cases) — the specific conditions
   that would make the scheme controllable: hours by area and day, capacity fixed by number and
   approved layout, a boundary noise limit with metric and measurement position, plant limits
   relative to background, delivery and servicing hours, verification testing before first use.
9. **Objection sentence** + sign-off.

## Each point: the anatomy

1. **Name the defect** in the heading as a conclusion ("The assessment reports no maximum noise
   levels at any receptor").
2. **Quote the document** — the applicant's own words with the paragraph, table, figure or
   appendix reference (`§6.4`, `Table 7.1`, `Appendix C`). Quoting the assessment against itself
   is the strongest move available. Two variants worth hunting for:
   - the **scope-versus-body** gap — the methodology section promises something the body never
     delivers;
   - the **appendix-versus-conclusion** gap — the data contains levels the narrative never
     mentions.
3. **Say why it matters** in planning terms — the consequence for the decision and the specific
   policy or standard engaged, cited precisely (P3(2)(b), P3(2)(d), P4(3), DM6(1)(c), BS
   4142:2014+A1:2019, PPG *Reference ID: 30-006-20190722*).
4. **State the ask** — the specific thing to do and *when* ("before determination", "secured by
   condition with a pre-use trigger").
5. **Say what it supports** — (A) refusal, (B) not yet determinable, (C) conditionable. Make
   this explicit somewhere in the point or the summary; it is what makes the representation
   usable rather than merely critical.

## Load-bearing arguments (use where they fit)

- **"Resolve by evidence, not by condition."** Where the gap goes to whether the effect is
  acceptable at all, a condition requiring a further assessment *after* permission defers the
  question the permission decides.
- **"The permission runs with the land, not the operator."** Against reliance on management
  measures, corporate policies and staff supervision.
- **"The modelled operation is not the permitted operation."** Against an assessment of a
  narrower scheme than the permission would allow.
- **"The uncertainty exceeds the margin."** Where the report's own stated error band is wider
  than the headroom its conclusion depends on — a point made entirely from the applicant's
  document.
- **"The standard was named, not applied."** Selective compliance with a methodology the
  assessment itself invokes.
- **"Predicted then; measured since."** Where the same method's predictions can be compared with
  later measurements at comparable sites — the strongest evidential move, and the one to get
  exactly right or leave out.

## Using other people's evidence

Noise cases lean on third-party material more than most: neighbours' accounts, complaint
records, consultee responses on other applications, appeal decisions. Rules:

- **Attribute everything** — document, author, date, application reference. An unattributed
  quote is worthless and looks worse.
- **Use the minimum third-party personal information needed**, and don't reproduce other
  people's names, addresses or personal circumstances beyond what the point requires. Your
  representation will be published.
- **Argue this application on its own facts.** Comparative material is context for the weight to
  be given to a *method*; it is not a substitute for critiquing the document in front of you,
  and a representation that reads as a campaign against an operator will be discounted.
- **Appeal decisions are not binding precedent** — they are evidence of how the tests have been
  applied. Say so when you cite one.
- **Never assert a decibel figure you cannot source.** If no number exists, the absence is the
  point.

## Length, formatting, and what to leave out

- **Shorter is stronger.** One point per paragraph; cut throat-clearing; if a sentence doesn't
  add a fact or a consequence, delete it. Prefer short sentences to long ones chained with
  semicolons.
- **Break dense material out — don't run it into prose.** This is what keeps the representation
  scannable:
  - where a point lists several things (the omitted metrics, the unassessed sources, the
    missing model inputs), use a **bulleted list**, not a semicolon-run inside a paragraph;
  - where a point has two or more distinct limbs, use **bold lettered sub-points**
    `(a)/(b)/(c)`;
  - present the framework list as bullets, and the requested conditions as a numbered list;
  - keep paragraphs short — a busy officer should see each point's structure at a glance.
- Bold each sub-section heading as a conclusion; italicise quoted document text; keep bullets
  parallel and short; end each point with a **Request:** line.
- **Handle numbers carefully.** State the metric, the period and the location every time — "73
  dB LAeq at the garden boundary" not "73 dB". Spell out an acronym once (NIA, NSR, LAeq,
  LAmax, LOAEL, SOAEL). Never round in the direction that helps you.
- British spelling and legal register ("the Council", "determination", "material
  consideration"). Reference documents precisely by author and date the first time ("the Noise
  Impact Assessment ([consultant], [date])").
- **Leave out:** anything you can't evidence; objections to the clientele or the operator;
  competition and property values; construction-period disturbance argued as a reason for
  refusal rather than a management-plan condition; padding and repetition; and any decibel
  figure you have estimated yourself.
