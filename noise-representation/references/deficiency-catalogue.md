# Noise submission — deficiency catalogue

The recurring, defensible grounds on which a planning application's **noise evidence** (a
Noise Impact Assessment, acoustic report, BS 4142 assessment, plant schedule, or noise
management plan) can be challenged. Each entry is a *pattern to look for*, not a template
sentence: find the specific instance, quote it, and attach the request. Cross-references
point to [`national-guidance.md`](national-guidance.md).

> **Integrity rule (read first).** Only raise a deficiency that is actually present and
> material. If the survey is at the right receptor over a representative period, the right
> standard is applied in full, maximum levels and character are addressed, the assumptions
> are justified, and the mitigation is specified and conditionable, there is **no sustainable
> objection** — say so.
>
> **The direction-of-error check — run it on every technical point before you write it.**
> Work out **which way the error you are alleging would move the answer**, and confirm it moves
> it against you. This is not pedantry; objectors and authorities alike lose points by getting
> it backwards. The standing example: attacking a baseline survey as unrepresentative because
> it was taken at a quiet time. A *higher* baseline makes the development's noise a *smaller*
> relative change and helps the applicant — so a complaint that the baseline is understated is
> the applicant's argument, made for them. The complaint that bites is almost always that the
> baseline is **overstated** (**A2**, **A3**), because that is what understates the change.
>
> For every point ask: *if this error were corrected, would the predicted impact go up or
> down?* If it would go down, cut the point. If you cannot tell, you do not yet understand the
> assessment well enough to attack it there.
>
> **Four anchors for noise objections:**
> - **The evidential burden is on the applicant.** This is the proposition everything else in
>   this catalogue rests on, and it is worth stating first because objectors routinely assume
>   the opposite — that they must prove harm. They must not. The applicant must demonstrate
>   acceptability, and where the evidence does not allow the decision-maker to reach that
>   conclusion, the deficiency is the applicant's problem, not the objector's. Inspectors state
>   it directly: that the evidential burden rests with the appellant to show the proposal would
>   not give rise to adverse effects, rather than with the council to demonstrate that harm
>   would occur. Frame every **(B)** finding this way.
> - **The environmental health officer is the ally when they object — and their silence is not
>   the end of it.** Environmental health is the internal consultee whose remit this is; read
>   their response first, and where they have made a point, evidence and reinforce it. But a
>   **"no objection" is not determinative and does not mean there is no case.** Appeals are
>   regularly decided against a development on noise where environmental health did not object,
>   for two reasons an objector can use: the consultee may have applied a **statutory nuisance**
>   test, which is a different and higher threshold than the planning question about living
>   conditions; and the planning judgement about what occupiers can reasonably expect is the
>   decision-maker's, not the consultee's. Where environmental health has not objected, say what
>   test they appear to have applied and why the planning question is broader — do not treat it
>   as fatal, and do not misrepresent it either.
> - **Attack the method, not the decibel — but do not conclude you have nothing to say without
>   data.** An objector has no measurements, and the winnable technical ground is that the
>   assessment does not answer the question **P3** asks. That does not make first-hand evidence
>   worthless: the absence of empirical evidence does not prevent a finding of harm, and
>   decision-makers routinely decide noise cases on **their own observation** at a site visit,
>   on the character of what they heard, and on residents' accounts of what they experience.
>   So describe, concretely and without exaggeration, **what is audible now and what will be
>   audible**, at which window, at what time of day — and ask the officer or inspector to visit
>   **at the time the noise occurs**, saying when that is. Expect the "a site visit is only a
>   snapshot" answer, and pre-empt it by putting on the file the evidence that what they will
>   hear is typical: dated notes, a log kept over weeks, several neighbours' consistent
>   accounts.
> - **Direction discipline.** Sections A–E and G apply in both directions. Section F is
>   **Direction B only** (new noise-sensitive development near an existing activity) — citing
>   P4 in a Direction A objection argues against your own case.

Sections: **A** scope and baseline · **B** method and metrics · **C** operation and worst case
· **D** mitigation and control · **E** evidence and credibility · **F** agent of change
(Direction B) · **G** procedure and conditions.

**The seven that most often decide the point**, if you read nothing else: **A1** (no assessment
at all) · **E6** (the report contradicts another of the applicant's documents) · **G5** (the
assessment deferred to a condition) · **D3** (reliance on management measures) · **D4** (the
case depends on windows being shut) · **C9** (loss of respite) · **B7** (character never
assessed). The first three need no acoustic competence whatever.

## A. Scope and baseline

**A1 — No noise assessment where one is needed.** A use, plant or hours change with an
obvious noise pathway to a nearby sensitive receptor, and nothing in the submission assesses
it.
- *Why it matters:* **Annex C** to the Framework lists, against **P3**, "such information as
  required to demonstrate compliance with relevant aspects of Policy P3". Without it the
  Council cannot make the P3 judgement at all.
- *Ask:* a noise assessment by a competent person, to a stated methodology, **before
  determination**. **(B)**

**A2 — Baseline measured in the wrong place.** The sound level meter sited where it suits the
result rather than where the receptor is — at the kerb of a busy road, on the far side of a
building, at ground level for an upper-floor bedroom.
- *Tell:* the survey location plan does not coincide with the receptor the assessment then
  reports levels *at*; or a measured LAeq dominated by a source (passing traffic) that does
  not reach the receptor in the same way.
- *Why it matters:* a baseline taken in a louder place **overstates** the existing
  environment and therefore **understates** the change the development would cause — the
  single most consequential error in the whole document, because every later conclusion rests
  on it.
- *Ask:* baseline measurements at, or properly corrected to, the actual receptor façades and
  amenity areas, with the location plan and justification. **(B)**

**A3 — Baseline includes the very activity being assessed, or another consented source.** The
"existing" survey is carried out while the use, the outdoor area or the plant is operating —
including where it operates under a temporary permission, a lapsed concession or without
permission at all. The related and broader case: the baseline includes a **neighbouring
consented development** whose own noise is then treated as part of the existing environment.
- *Tell:* the survey dates coincide with the activity running; the report acknowledges a nearby
  installation was operating; a background level that is surprisingly high for the setting, with
  no explanation of what is producing it.
- *Why it matters:* it builds the impact into the baseline and then reports little or no
  change. Where the activity is unlawful or time-limited, the correct baseline is the lawful
  fallback position — and a source permitted only temporarily should not be inflating a
  background against which permanent development is then judged.
- ***Noise creep* — name it, because naming it is what makes the point land.** Each permission
  measured against a background already raised by the last one narrows the apparent difference,
  so each successive development is assessed as a smaller change than it is, and the ratchet
  runs one way only. That is the systemic reason a contaminated baseline is not a technical
  quibble, and it is language decision-makers recognise. Pairs with the "watch the ratchet"
  framing rule in the SKILL.
- *A proxy location is a legitimate answer.* Where the activity cannot be turned off for a
  survey, measuring at an equivalent unaffected location is a reasonable approach and you should
  not attack it as such — attack it only if the proxy is not genuinely equivalent.
- *Ask:* a baseline representing the **lawful** existing situation with the assessed activity
  excluded, by survey when it is not operating or at a justified proxy location. **(B)**

**A4 — Survey too short, or over an unrepresentative period.** One day; weekends only (or
weekdays only); a single season; a period affected by atypical conditions (roadworks, a
closure, unusual weather, an emergency restriction, school holidays).
- *Tell:* the appendix shows a monitoring window of hours rather than days, or dates that
  coincide with something that makes them atypical; no justification of representativeness.
- *Why it matters:* the quietest periods — when the development's noise will be most
  noticeable — are precisely the ones a short or badly-timed survey misses.
- *Ask:* survey data covering the periods when the development would operate *and* the
  quietest periods a receptor experiences, with representativeness justified. **(B)**

**A5 — The statistics show a variable environment, and the assessment ignores it.** Ambient
levels far above background (a large LAeq−L90 gap), or a daytime L10 well above the LAeq, are
the signature of an environment dominated by short loud events with quiet periods between.
- *Why it matters:* in such an environment an average is a poor description of what a
  resident experiences. A development whose noise sits below the *average* can still be
  plainly audible, and intrusive, in the quiet periods between events.
- *Ask:* assessment against the quieter periods the receptor actually experiences, not the
  averaged ambient. **(B)**

**A6 — No attended measurements, no observations, no raw data.** Unattended logging only, with
no record of what was happening, how many people were present, or what the meter was
capturing; raw data and statistical output not provided for scrutiny.
- *Why it matters:* without observations nobody can say whether the measurement represents a
  typical condition, a worst case, or an empty site — so it cannot validate anything. And a
  conclusion whose underlying data is withheld cannot be checked by the Council or its
  consultee.
- *Ask:* attended measurements with observation logs and occupancy records, and the raw
  time-history data and statistical analysis provided. **(B)**

**A7 — Wrong or incomplete receptors.** The assessed "nearest sensitive receptor" is not the
nearest or the most exposed; upper-floor windows, rear elevations, gardens, balconies and
non-residential sensitive receptors (schools, nurseries, care homes, hospitals, places of
worship) omitted.
- *Tell:* a receptor plan that skips the property closest to the source, or reports a façade
  that faces away from it; ground-floor only.
- *Why it matters:* P3 asks about effects on living conditions at the receptors that exist —
  and a mis-selected receptor can understate exposure by a wide margin. Gardens and balconies
  matter in their own right: the PPG treats outdoor amenity space as part of the amenity to
  be protected.
- *Ask:* assessment at the nearest and most exposed receptors, including upper floors and
  external amenity areas, with the selection justified. **(B)**

**A8 — The noise-generating element is characterised as ancillary.** The application presents
the loud component as subordinate to a quieter primary use — a bar "ancillary" to a hotel, a
function room "ancillary" to a restaurant, a taproom "ancillary" to a brewery — and the
assessment then scopes its source terms to the primary use.
- *Tell:* the description and the planning statement emphasise the primary use, while the
  drawings show the "ancillary" element with substantial seating, its own frontage, its own
  entrance, or its own hours; the assessment's source terms account for the primary use only.
- *Why it matters:* **scope is decided before anything is measured**, so a mischaracterisation
  here removes the principal source from the assessment without anyone having to argue about
  decibels. **P3(1)** directs the decision-maker to the likely effects of the development, not
  to the label the applicant has given it. And a use that is genuinely ancillary today can
  intensify to the point of becoming a primary use without a further application, unless the
  permission controls it — so "ancillary" is a description that has to be *secured*, not
  accepted.
- *Ask:* the element assessed at the capacity the drawings and the application form actually
  show; and, where ancillary status is genuinely relied on, that status fixed by condition
  (floor area, covers, hours, independent access). **(B)**, with **(C)** on the securing point.

**A9 — The assessment answers the wrong direction.** A Direction B assessment (the ambient
environment's effect on the new dwellings) submitted where the issue is Direction A (the
development's effect on existing neighbours), or the reverse. A scoping defect, not a method
defect — the work may be competent and simply not address the question.
- *Tell:* a report whose receptors are all *inside* the red line where the objection is about
  the neighbours, or one that assesses only **breakout from the building** where the concern is
  the activity at the access; a report that models road traffic onto the site where the
  council's reason for refusal is comings and goings off it.
- *Why it matters:* an assessment of the wrong direction is not weak evidence on the right
  question, it is **no evidence at all** on the right question — so the burden is undischarged
  however good the document is. It is also easy to miss, because a substantial, professional
  report creates the impression the topic has been covered.
- *Ask:* state which direction the policy requires here and ask that the assessment address it.
  **(B)**

**A10 — Only the façade pathway assessed: structure-borne and flanking transmission ignored.**
The assessment models airborne noise arriving at the outside of the building and stops. Where
the source is in, under, above or attached to the same structure — a bar below a flat, a plant
room on the other side of a party wall, a lift shaft, a service riser — the pathway that
matters runs **through the structure**, and façade mitigation does nothing about it.
- *Tell:* a report that discusses glazing and ventilator specifications but contains no internal
  measurements, no testing of the separating construction, and no assessment of flanking paths
  at junctions; professional judgement about the "likely mass" of a party wall offered in place
  of a test.
- *Why it matters:* the conclusion rests on an assumption about a building element nobody has
  measured, and the mitigation proposed is aimed at a different pathway from the one carrying
  the noise. Upgrading the windows cannot fix a floor.
- *Ask:* site-specific testing of the separating structure and the flanking paths, with internal
  measurements — not professional judgement about likely construction. **(B)**

**A11 — Area-wide characterisation applied to a receptor in a quieter pocket.** The assessment
(or the planning statement) characterises the acoustic context by the area's label — "town
centre", "mixed use", "a vibrant quarter", "adjacent to a busy road" — and reasons from that to
what the receptor can expect.
- *Tell:* "the site lies within a town centre where noise is part of the prevailing character",
  with no measurement or observation at the receptor's own position; a designation doing the
  work that a survey should.
- *Why it matters:* this is **the commonest single argument applicants run**, and the
  commonest way it is defeated is by distinguishing the specific façade from the area. Streets a
  few metres apart differ sharply — a frontage on the main road and a rear elevation onto a
  side street are not in the same acoustic environment, and an area characterisation smooths
  exactly the difference the receptor lives in. Note this is distinct from **A2**: the
  measurements may be sound and the *characterisation* still wrong.
- *Ask:* the acoustic context established at the receptor's own position and orientation, by
  measurement or recorded observation, rather than inferred from the area's designation. **(B)**

**A12 — Enclosure, reflection and reverberation at the receiving space not addressed.** Noise
behaves differently in a narrow street, a courtyard, a lightwell or a space enclosed on three
sides: sound reflects, reverberates and carries to upper floors rather than dissipating.
- *Tell:* a prediction using free-field assumptions or simple distance attenuation for a
  receptor in a tightly enclosed street or yard; no account of reflection from facing façades.
- *Also run it in reverse:* where an activity is **moved from an enclosed space to an open
  one** — from inside a building to a terrace, from a walled yard to an open platform — treat
  that as an intensification, not a neutral relocation. Applicants present such moves as
  reducing pressure on the original space; the noise now propagates where it previously did not.
- *Ask:* reflection, reverberation and enclosure at the receiving street or courtyard assessed,
  including at upper floors. **(B)**

## B. Method and metrics

**B1 — Averages only: no maximum levels.** The assessment reports LAeq and stops. No LAmax,
no analysis of the loudest events, no commentary on their source.
- *Tell:* tables of LAeq with no LAmax column; or LAmax values present in an appendix or a
  case-study dataset and never discussed in the text.
- *Why it matters:* for intermittent sources — voices, shouting, cheering, glass, doors,
  vehicle movements, plant cycling, deliveries — the discrete loud event is what wakes people
  and what generates complaints. An assessment silent on maximum levels has not assessed the
  effect that actually occurs. This is the single most common finding environmental health
  officers make on assessments of this type.
- *Ask:* maximum levels predicted and assessed at the receptors, for the periods when the use
  would operate, including after dark. **(B)**

**B2 — An averaging period long enough to hide the event.** A 16-hour daytime average applied
to a use that operates for a few hours in the evening; an hourly LAeq covering a period
containing a short intense episode.
- *Why it matters:* averaging a concentrated evening activity across the whole day dilutes it
  to a number that bears no relation to what a resident hears at 9pm. The longer the
  averaging period relative to the activity, the more the assessment understates it.
- *Ask:* assessment over periods matched to the hours the activity would actually operate,
  including a separate evening and night-time assessment where the use extends into them.
  **(B)**

**B3 — Guidance for steady, continuous sources applied to intermittent or human-source noise.**
Criteria derived for road, rail, aircraft or plant noise — anonymous, steady, predictable —
applied without adjustment to voices, crowds, amplified sound, impacts or other variable
sources.
- *Tell:* a WHO community-noise annoyance figure, or a transport-derived criterion, adopted as
  *the* acceptability threshold for a fundamentally different kind of noise, with no
  discussion of its applicability.
- *Why it matters:* the guidance itself is explicit that care is needed where noise has a
  particular character, and the PPG lists the character and the "distinguishability" of the
  source among the factors that influence whether noise is a concern. Human voice is the
  paradigm case: it is intelligible, attention-getting, unpredictable in level, and cannot be
  tuned out the way a constant background can.
- *Ask:* the criterion justified as appropriate for **this** source, or an appropriate
  assessment method adopted. **(B)**
- **⚠️ "The threshold is borrowed" is not by itself an argument, and it fails on its own.** A
  report that names its sources — a British Standard, WHO guidance — and derives its
  acceptability criteria from them has done something orthodox, and a decision-maker will want
  to know **what is different about this source** that makes those criteria inapt. Saying the
  criterion came from somewhere else is not enough; the objector who cannot articulate the
  mismatch loses the point. Name the characteristic the borrowed criterion does not capture —
  intelligibility of speech, sudden onset, tonality, unpredictability, the absence of any
  habituation — and say why it matters at this receptor. The *Ask* above is the operative part
  of this entry, not the tell.

**B4 — Cherry-picked methodology.** The report names a recognised methodology, takes one
number or one step from it, and ignores the rest of what that methodology requires.
- *Tell:* "assessed in accordance with [methodology]" followed by a single change-in-level
  figure, where the same methodology also requires consideration of character, maximum and
  underlying levels, context, the nature of the change and the sensitivity of the receptor.
- *Why it matters:* a methodology's numeric criteria are not severable from its qualitative
  requirements. Selective compliance produces a conclusion the methodology itself does not
  support — and stating a methodology and then not following it is a defect the Council can
  point to on the face of the document.
- *Ask:* the stated methodology applied **in full**, or a different one adopted and justified.
  **(B)**

**B5 — The method is unjustified.** Commercial or industrial sound (plant, extraction,
refrigeration, servicing) assessed without reference to **BS 4142**; residential internal and
external targets addressed without **BS 8233**; a noise-sensitive residential scheme assessed
without **ProPG** or the **AVO Guide** — *and no reasoned explanation given for the method
actually used*.
- **Lead with the justification limb, not the named-standard limb.** The deficiency is an
  **unjustified** method, not the use of a method other than the one you expected. An objection
  that says "BS 4142 should have been used" and stops there loses where the consultant has
  taken a different but reasoned route — measuring the actual insulation performance of the
  building envelope and accounting for the frequency content of the source, for instance, can
  be a perfectly proper alternative, and an inspector will say so. Ask **why this method, for
  this source, at this receptor**, and attack the answer or its absence.
- *Two things that follow, and both matter:*
  - **A standard can be the wrong instrument as well as a missing one.** Where the noise has a
    marked character — tonality above all — a standard built around ambient levels may be
    positively inapt, and saying which standard does *not* fit is as strong a point as saying
    which one does.
  - **Distinguish the instruments' status.** BS 4142 and BS 8233 are British Standards. **ProPG
    is not government guidance**, does not replace or authoritatively interpret policy, and has
    been described in those terms by inspectors — so asking for "compliance with ProPG" as
    though it were mandatory over-claims. It is given real weight where its relevance is agreed
    or argued; argue it, don't assume it.
- *Ask:* the method justified as appropriate for **this** source, context and receptor — or an
  appropriate method adopted. **(B)**

**B6 — BS 4142 applied incorrectly.** Where it is used: the background (LA90) determined over
the wrong period or in the wrong place; **no acoustic feature correction** for tonality,
impulsivity, intermittency or other distinguishing characteristics; the context section
skipped; **uncertainty** not quantified.
- *Tell:* a "rating level" identical to the specific sound level (i.e. a +0 dB correction)
  for a source with obvious character; a margin of exceedance smaller than the assessment's
  own stated uncertainty.
- *Why it matters:* the corrections and the context discussion are where BS 4142 does its
  work. Omitting them turns a rating assessment into a bare arithmetic comparison, and can
  move the answer by several decibels — often more than the whole margin the conclusion rests
  on.
- *Ask:* a compliant BS 4142 assessment with feature corrections, context and uncertainty
  addressed. **(B)**

**B7 — The character of the noise never assessed.** Nothing on tonality, impulsivity,
intermittency, distinguishability, intelligibility, low-frequency content, or how the noise
will be *experienced* as against merely measured.
- *Why it matters:* both national guidance and the standards treat character as integral, not
  optional — and the courts have recognised in the statutory-nuisance context that the
  character of noise matters, not merely its absolute level (see `national-guidance.md` on
  *Roper v Tussauds Theme Parks*, persuasive on the point though not a planning authority).
  Two sources at the same measured level can have entirely different effects.
- *Ask:* the character of the noise assessed, and its effect on the receptors addressed
  qualitatively as well as numerically. **(B)**

**B8 — Modelling assumptions unstated, implausible, or unvalidated.** Source terms not given;
a crowd modelled as a single point source or from a single-speaker sound power level; source
heights, directivity, ground and screening effects unstated; the model never validated against
measurement.
- *Tell:* contour plots with no accompanying table of inputs; a stated sound power level with
  no derivation; "the method has been validated" with nothing cited.
- *Why it matters:* a prediction is only as good as its inputs, and inputs that are not
  disclosed cannot be checked. A dispersed crowd, whose individual voices are separately
  audible and intelligible, does not behave like one point source.
- *Ask:* full model inputs, assumptions and derivations disclosed, and validation evidence
  provided. **(B)**

**B9 — Uncertainty ignored, or stated and then disregarded.** No uncertainty analysis; or an
uncertainty of ±3–4 dB stated in the method section, and a conclusion that rests on a
predicted margin smaller than it.
- *Why it matters:* where the stated uncertainty exceeds the margin, the assessment's own
  figures do not support its conclusion. This is a point that can be made from the document
  alone, without any competing measurement — which makes it one of the strongest available to
  a lay objector.
- *Ask:* the conclusion re-stated against the range the uncertainty permits, and the worst
  case within that range assessed. **(B)** — and where the worst case is clearly unacceptable,
  **(A)**.

**B10 — No LOAEL or SOAEL identified.** The assessment never states, for this source and this
context, the level at which adverse effects begin (LOAEL) or become significant (SOAEL) — it
simply compares a predicted level to a borrowed number.
- *Why it matters:* **P3(2)(d)** requires that development "not result in levels of noise
  exposure which would have a **significant observed adverse effect**", and the Framework's
  glossary defines the significant observed adverse effect level. The PPG sets out the
  effect-level hierarchy and what should happen at each. An assessment that does not identify
  where those thresholds lie for this noise cannot demonstrate the policy test is met, and the
  officer report cannot record that it does.
- *Ask:* the LOAEL and SOAEL adopted for this source and context, justified, and the predicted
  exposure assessed against them. **(B)**

**B11 — External levels reported, internal levels never stated.** The assessment predicts
levels at the façade and stops there, so nothing in it says what a resident would hear in the
room — which is the level the residential guideline values are expressed in.
- *Tell:* a table of external levels at the receptor with no corresponding internal figures;
  or internal levels given for the **closed-window** condition only, with no open-window case.
- *Why it matters:* the internal level is what **P3(2)(a)** and the residential guidance are
  about, and the step from one to the other depends entirely on an assumed façade attenuation
  — which in turn depends on whether windows are open or shut. An assessment that leaves the
  step out has not made the comparison the policy requires, and an assessment that makes it
  silently for the closed-window condition has assumed away the ventilation question (**D4**).
- *Care — and this is a hard limit:* **do not perform the conversion yourself.** This skill
  critiques acoustics; it does not produce them, and an objector who supplies their own
  arithmetic invites the whole representation to be dismissed on it. The point is the
  **absence** of the figure and of the stated assumption behind it, argued as such. Where a
  consultee has done the conversion, cite the consultee.
- *Ask:* internal levels stated at the affected rooms for **both** the open-window and
  closed-window conditions, with the assumed façade attenuation stated and justified. **(B)**

**B12 — The authority's own criterion ignored, or borrowed out of its context.** Two opposite
errors, both common, and they are worth checking together because the same local document
usually produces both.
- *Limb 1 — ignored.* The authority publishes its own noise criteria (a plant limit expressed
  as a margin below background, survey requirements, standard condition wordings), and the
  assessment adopts a different and more permissive threshold without mentioning the local
  document at all.
- *Limb 2 — borrowed out of context.* The assessment **does** cite the authority's published
  effect levels, but those figures were derived for a different kind of source — most often
  **transport noise** — and are applied without adjustment to patron, plant or commercial
  noise. Expect the applicant's technical response to argue that the local guidance is not
  expressed as source-specific; the answer is that a criterion derived from transport-noise
  dose-response evidence does not become applicable to human-source noise by being silent
  about its provenance.
- *Why it matters:* the authority's own guidance is the Council's own document, it is usually
  more specific than anything national, and a departure from it is a departure the case officer
  must explain. **PPG 30-007** expressly contemplates noise standards in local policies, and
  **30-006** makes character and distinguishability factors in whether noise is a concern.
- *Check the local document's provenance before you rely on it.* Local guidance is not all of
  equal weight, and this cuts against the objector as readily as for them. Guidance that has
  been **consulted on and formally adopted** — as an SPD, or as part of the development plan —
  carries real weight. An **in-house technical note issued by a council department without any
  consultation, scrutiny or adoption process** may be given only limited weight, and a
  decision-maker may prefer a well-established national standard to it. So establish what the
  document is before building a point on it, and where it is unadopted, run the point on the
  *substance* of the criterion rather than on the authority's obligation to follow its own
  guidance.
- *Ask:* the local criterion applied, or the departure from it justified; and where local
  figures are relied on, their applicability to **this** source demonstrated. **(B)**

**B13 — The maximum level assessed is a selected rank from the dataset, and the selection is
unexplained.** The survey records a range of maximum events; the assessment picks one — the
second highest, the tenth highest, the "typical" maximum — and assesses against that. The
highest measured events are never assessed at all.
- *Tell:* a table showing a wide L<sub>Amax</sub> range, and an assessment section that
  silently uses one value from within it; a footnote saying which rank was used, with no reason
  given. **The strongest version of this tell: a *different* rank used for different scenarios
  — a higher one for the windows-closed case and a lower one for the windows-open case**, which
  flatters exactly the scenario most likely to fail.
- *Why it matters:* selecting the statistic selects the answer, and the selection is a
  judgement that has to be justified like any other. Discarding the loudest events is precisely
  discarding the events that wake people — and where the discarded maximum is many decibels
  above the one assessed, the assessment has not addressed the effect that actually occurs.
  This is checkable on the face of the document, needs no competing measurement, and is
  arithmetic an objector can point at without doing any.
- *Ask:* the statistic used stated and justified, applied **consistently across every
  scenario**, and the highest measured events assessed. **(B)**

## C. Operation, occupancy and worst case

**C1 — Unjustified occupancy and usage assumptions.** A stated proportion of capacity, or of
the time, occupied — with no evidence for it.
- *Tell:* "the area is assumed to be at 50% occupancy for 60% of the operating period" or
  similar, with no survey, no comparator data, no sensitivity test.
- *Why it matters:* occupancy drives the source term directly. An assumption chosen without
  evidence, in the direction that helps the applicant, is not a worst case — and warm evenings
  and weekends, when outdoor areas fill and windows open, are exactly when the assumption
  fails and the impact is greatest.
- *Ask:* the assumption evidenced, and the assessment re-run at **full capacity** in the
  conditions when the area will actually be busiest. **(B)**

**C2 — Capacity not fixed by anything.** The assessment models N patrons, seats or covers, but
nothing in the application would limit the numbers actually present.
- *Why it matters:* a prediction premised on a capacity the permission does not control is
  worthless the day after approval — and outdoor areas are routinely re-laid out to hold more
  than the drawing showed.
- *Ask:* the modelled capacity fixed by condition (numbers and the approved layout), or the
  assessment re-run at the maximum the space can physically hold. **(C)**

**C3 — No worst case.** One scenario modelled — typically a moderate one — with no warm
evening, no weekend or event day, no all-plant-running case, and no sensitivity analysis.
- *Ask:* a reasonable worst-case scenario assessed and reported. **(B)**

**C4 — The modelled operation is not the permitted operation.** The assessment models a
pattern of use narrower than the permission would allow — earlier closing, indoor use only,
doors closed, fewer delivery movements — with no condition tying the permission to it.
- *Why it matters:* permission is granted for what the description and conditions permit, not
  for what the acoustic report assumed. Any gap between them is unassessed impact.
- *Ask:* the permission tied by condition to the modelled operation, or the assessment re-run
  on the operation actually applied for. **(C)**, escalating to **(B)** where the gap is large.

**C5 — Displacement and dispersal not assessed.** When an outdoor area closes, or at closing
time, people move — into another external area, onto the frontage, into the street, to the car
park. Smoking areas, queuing, taxi pick-ups and vehicles starting up are part of the noise the
development generates.
- *Tell:* a curfew on one area presented as mitigation, with no assessment of where the
  occupants go, or of the concentration effect on the area that stays open.
- *Why it matters:* dispersal noise is late, close to bedrooms, and characteristically loud
  and intermittent. A curfew that merely relocates people is not mitigation.
- *Ask:* the effects of displacement, concentration and dispersal assessed at the receptors,
  for the hours applied for. **(B)**

**C6 — Servicing, deliveries, waste and plant left out.** Deliveries, bottle and waste
collection, cellar drops, refrigeration and extraction plant, kitchen ventilation, condensers,
external doors, and their timing — omitted, or covered by a sentence.
- *Tell:* a report about patron noise that never assesses the plant schedule; no assessment of
  early-morning collections; an hours change assessed for patrons only.
- *Why it matters:* these are among the most complained-of sources at operating premises,
  they occur at the most sensitive hours, and they are among the most readily conditioned —
  which makes them worth raising even when the rest of the assessment survives.
- *Ask:* servicing, delivery, waste-collection and plant noise assessed and, where acceptable
  in principle, hours and specifications secured by condition. **(B)/(C)**

**C7 — Cumulative effects not assessed.** The development's noise considered in isolation
from other sources, other consented development, or the other elements of the same scheme.
- *Why it matters:* **P3(1)** expressly requires the likely effects of pollution to be
  considered "including cumulative effects", "whether these effects are on or off-site", and
  "whether this is as a result of the development itself or the product of pre-existing
  conditions in its vicinity".
- *Ask:* a cumulative assessment covering the scheme's own sources together and the other
  relevant sources affecting the same receptors. **(B)**

**C8 — Plant assessed as new, and only as new.** The prediction rests on the manufacturer's
sound power data for equipment in commissioning condition, with no allowance for how it will
perform across the life of the permission and no maintenance regime secured.
- *Tell:* a plant schedule of catalogue figures with no derating, no in-service allowance, and
  no commitment to maintenance; a **BS 4142** assessment finding no acoustic feature to correct
  for, based on equipment that has not yet been installed.
- *Why it matters:* the acoustic features that attract a correction — tonality above all, but
  also impulsivity and intermittency — characteristically *emerge* as equipment wears: bearings,
  fan mountings, anti-vibration mounts and dampers. A permission is granted for the life of the
  development, not for its first month, and an assessment that only ever describes new plant has
  not addressed the condition in which the plant will spend most of its life. The point pairs
  with **D2**: the maintenance regime is exactly the kind of measure that is relied on in the
  conclusion and secured nowhere.
- *Ask:* the reasonable worst case to include an in-service plant condition; and a condition
  requiring the plant to be maintained to the assessed specification, with verification testing
  after installation and a mechanism for re-testing if a tonal or impulsive character develops.
  **(B)** on the assessment, **(C)** on the securing.

**C9 — Loss of respite: the periods of guaranteed quiet are never assessed.** On any
application to **extend hours**, the assessment addresses the level during the new hours and
says nothing about what is lost — the reliable, predictable periods in which the receptors
currently have relief.
- *Why it matters, and this is the most under-used argument in the catalogue:* the protected
  interest is not only the level, it is the **predictability**. Residents who know an activity
  runs to 6pm on weekdays and not at all on Sundays can plan around it; the harm of an extension
  is that the quiet periods they organise their lives around disappear. That harm is real at
  levels well below any threshold, and it is **not answered by an acoustic assessment at all** —
  which is precisely its strength. It needs no measurements, no competing expert and no
  arithmetic; it needs the current permitted hours and the proposed ones, side by side.
- *Tell:* an assessment that reports predicted levels during the extended period and never
  identifies what the existing pattern of relief is; an application that removes a weekend or
  evening closure without mentioning it.
- *Where it is strongest:* evenings, weekends, Sundays and bank holidays — when people are most
  at home, most in their gardens and least able to go elsewhere — and where the existing
  restriction was itself imposed to secure that relief.
- *Ask:* the periods of respite the receptors currently enjoy identified, and the effect of
  removing or reducing them assessed in its own right. Where hours are extended, ask for the
  relief to be preserved explicitly — a closure day, a seasonal differentiation, a guaranteed
  quiet period — rather than for refusal of the whole. **(B)**, and **(C)** on the ask.

**C10 — The current operator's restraint treated as the level of use.** The assessment, or the
applicant's statement, rests on how the business is run *now* — appointment-only, sporadic,
low-key, seasonal, "we only do this occasionally" — where nothing in the permission would hold
it there.
- *Tell:* "the use operates at a low level of intensity"; "the extended hours would be used only
  occasionally, in summer"; reliance on the applicant personally living on site, or on a
  particular management style, or on the goodwill of a proprietor.
- *Why it matters:* this is **C2 and C4's cousin, and distinct from both** — C2 is about
  capacity not being fixed, C4 about a gap between the modelled and the permitted operation.
  This is about voluntary restraint by the current operator being read as a property of the
  development. It is not. A permission is exercised at the intensity it allows, by whoever holds
  it, and the personal circumstances of the present proprietor carry no weight. Expect the
  "seasonal and occasional" answer and ask what in the permission would make it so.
- *Ask:* the assessment run at the intensity the permission would allow; and where the restraint
  genuinely matters to acceptability, fixed by condition rather than assumed. **(B)/(C)**

**C11 — No defined operation, so nothing can be assessed.** Distinct from **C4**, where a
defined operation is narrower than the permission: here there is **no operating model at all**.
A flexible use-class permission, a change of use with no layout, hours or staffing stated, an
"up to" scheme, or an outline application with noise left to reserved matters.
- *Tell:* an application for a broad use class with no layout or operational details; an
  acoustic report prepared for one specific use offered in support of a permission that would
  allow a range of others; an outline application with a noise limit condition but no baseline.
- *Why it matters:* the decision-maker has to be satisfied that **any use the permission would
  allow** is acceptable, not merely the one the applicant has in mind. Where the range of
  permitted activity varies materially in customer numbers, staffing, servicing and hours, an
  assessment of one point within that range tells the authority nothing about the rest of it.
  At **outline** stage there is a further problem: noise mitigation constrains layout and can
  constrain capacity, so deferring it can mean the "up to" quantum is undeliverable — and the
  professional guidance for new residential development is explicit that outline permission
  should not be granted on a medium- or high-risk site without first being satisfied that
  acoustic design can overcome the challenge.
- *Ask:* the assessment run on the **most intensive use the permission would allow**, or the
  permission narrowed by condition to the use actually assessed; and at outline stage, enough
  assessment to show the mitigation is achievable at the quantum proposed. **(B)**

## D. Mitigation and control

**D1 — Mitigation assumed to perform perfectly.** An acoustic barrier, fence, canopy or
enclosure credited with a full theoretical insertion loss, with no acoustic specification (mass
per unit area, gaps, joints), no allowance for doors, gates or junctions with existing
structures, and no flanking or reflection analysis.
- *Tell:* a barrier attenuation figure with no specification behind it; a proposed structure
  with no doors shown, where means of escape will require them; no allowance for sound passing
  over, around or through it.
- *Why it matters:* barrier performance is highly sensitive to gaps and to the geometry of
  source and receiver — particularly for receptors at first-floor level and above, where a
  ground-level barrier may provide little benefit at all. Assumed performance that the built
  thing will not deliver is a prediction of a development that will not be built.
- *Ask:* the acoustic specification, the assumptions on gaps, doors and junctions, and
  performance assessed at **upper-floor** receptors as well as ground level. **(B)**

**D2 — Mitigation not secured, or not capable of being secured.** Measures relied on in the
conclusion that appear nowhere in the drawings, the description or the proposed conditions.
- **The named tell, and it is cheap to run: the applicant's own consultant recommended
  something the application does not include.** Read the acoustic report's recommendations
  section against the submitted drawings and the draft conditions, line by line. Where the
  consultant's own mitigation has been left out, the applicant cannot set it aside — their
  expert said it was needed, and the scheme before the authority does not have it. This needs
  no competing evidence at all, only a comparison of two documents the applicant supplied.
- **Check it *can* be secured, not just that it has not been.** A measure may be incapable of
  being conditioned: it is outside the red line, it would itself require a separate permission,
  it is not permitted development, or the class of application (a prior approval) does not
  permit the external alterations it would need. Where the conclusion depends on something that
  cannot lawfully be delivered under this permission, the mitigation is unavailable and the
  scheme is unmitigated — which is a stronger point than "it is not conditioned yet".
- *Ask:* every measure the conclusion relies on shown on the drawings and secured by condition,
  with a pre-occupation or pre-use trigger and a verification requirement — or, where it cannot
  be secured, the conclusion re-stated without it. **(C)**, escalating to **(B)/(A)** where the
  measure cannot be delivered.

**D3 — Reliance on operator management measures.** The conclusion rests on staff supervision,
a management plan, signage, "last orders" practice, or a corporate operating policy.
- *Why it matters:* three problems. **Lead with the first two — they are what decision-makers
  actually act on.**
  - **Staff cannot control what happens off the premises.** This is the point that wins. Once
    patrons are outside — on the street, in a smoking area, walking to a car or waiting for a
    taxi — staff have limited ability to control their noise, however well trained, and a
    measure that depends on doing so is not control at all. A small number of people behaving
    loudly is enough to cause disturbance, and the damage is done before anyone intervenes.
  - **The plan's contents are not monitorable or enforceable.** Management plans commit to
    things like the tone and volume of patrons' conversation, or to "encouraging" quiet
    departure. Those are not obligations a planning authority can monitor or enforce, and a
    measure that cannot be enforced is not mitigation (**DM6(1)(c)**). The same objection is
    fatal to a condition requiring the plan to be *submitted and agreed later*: deferring the
    detail defers the very question the decision has to answer, so the decision-maker cannot
    be satisfied the harm is prevented.
  - **Permission runs with the land, not the operator.** A permission granted on the strength
    of one operator's practices survives that operator's departure. This is legally sound and
    worth a line, but it is **the weakest of the three in practice** — it appears far less
    often in decisions than the two above. Do not lead with it.
  - Where the applicant relies on such measures at other sites, ask what the complaint record
    at those sites shows.
- *Ask:* the measures the conclusion relies on expressed as **precise, enforceable
  conditions** (**DM6(1)(c)**) — hours, capacity, a boundary noise limit, a closure and
  clearance regime — rather than as management commitments; and, where the effect cannot be
  controlled by condition, that limb of the proposal refused. **(C)**, escalating to **(A)**
  where the only proposed control is management.

**D4 — Mitigation that depends on the windows being shut.** (Direction B.) Internal noise
targets achieved only with windows closed. **This is a defect in itself, and the entry has two
limbs — the second is the stronger and is the one most often missed.**

- *Limb 1 — the ventilation and overheating case is missing.* Closed-window targets with no
  assessment of how the dwellings are ventilated or kept from overheating. The PPG is clear
  that mitigation should not make for an unsatisfactory development, and the AVO Guide and
  ProPG exist precisely because acoustics, ventilation and overheating have to be resolved
  together. **DP3(2)(b)** requires design that minimises overheating risk.
- *Limb 2 — the dependence is itself the objection, even where the numbers are met.* Do not
  concede the point merely because the ventilation strategy has been specified and secured.
  The decisive reasoning is about **control, not acoustics**: nobody — not the applicant, not
  the authority, not a condition — can determine whether an occupier opens a window. An
  occupier who opens one defeats the mitigation and is exposed to the unmitigated level; an
  occupier who does not is living in a sealed space reliant on mechanical ventilation. A
  scheme acceptable only on the second assumption does not provide a good standard of amenity,
  and it does not become acceptable because the occupier chose to live there. Expect, and
  rebut, both standard answers — that mechanical ventilation is provided (it does not stop
  people opening windows), and that occupiers will have known what they were buying (the
  permission runs with the land, the second occupier made no such choice, and a poor standard
  of amenity is not cured by consent to it).
- *The cross-check that makes this concrete, and it is often decisive:* compare the acoustic
  report with the **overheating or thermal comfort assessment** (a TM59 or Part O assessment).
  They routinely contradict each other — the thermal model achieves its result by assuming
  windows open through the day, while the NIA requires them closed. Both are the applicant's
  own documents and the contradiction is visible on their face. This is a named instance of
  **E6**; run it there and cross-refer.
- *Ask:* acceptable internal levels demonstrated with windows in their **normal open
  position**; or the scheme redesigned — layout, orientation, stacking, buffering — so that
  closing a window is a choice and not a precondition. Where the acoustic and thermal cases
  rest on different window assumptions, the two reconciled on a single stated assumption before
  determination. **(A)/(B)**

**D5 — External amenity space of the new development not assessed.** (Direction B.) Internal
levels addressed; gardens, balconies, courtyards, play space and shared amenity areas ignored.
- *It decides cases on its own.* Do not treat this as a makeweight behind the internal case.
  Schemes are refused on external amenity with the internal case **expressly conceded** — and
  a level at the **top** of the guideline range is treated as marginal rather than compliant,
  not as a pass.
- *The second failure mode, which is easy to miss: the mitigation ruins the space it protects.*
  Where the acoustic answer for a garden is a barrier, check what the barrier does to the
  garden. A fence tall enough to work can be dominating and enclosing; an "inner" acoustic
  fence set forward of the boundary subdivides the garden and cuts off the part behind it. A
  space that is quiet because it has been walled in, or halved, has not been made usable. So
  the ask is not only "assess it" but "demonstrate it is usable **after** mitigation".
- *Expect, and rebut, the "there is a park nearby" answer.* Public open space is not a
  substitute for private amenity space: it is used differently in frequency, privacy and
  security, and an unpleasant garden is not offset by a park.
- *Ask:* external amenity areas assessed against the applicable external guideline, **and** the
  usability of the space demonstrated with the proposed mitigation in place, at its actual
  scale. **(B)**

**D6 — "Another regime will deal with it."** The assessment, or the applicant's agent, answers
a noise concern by pointing to statutory nuisance powers, environmental permitting or the
licensing regime.
- *Two different answers are available, and they have completely different evidential
  requirements. Do not confuse them — the first is the one that usually works.*

  **(a) The scope answer — the other regime asks a narrower question. No evidence needed.**
  **P3(3)** and **DM7(1)** require the decision-maker to assume the other regime will *operate
  effectively*. They do not require an assumption that it asks the **same question**, and it
  does not. Each of the parallel regimes is aimed at a different and narrower target than
  planning's:
  - **statutory nuisance** asks whether noise is prejudicial to health or a nuisance — a
    materially higher threshold than planning's question about living conditions, so a level
    well below statutory nuisance can still be an unacceptable effect under **P3**;
  - **licensing** is directed at the prevention of public nuisance and the other licensing
    objectives, not at securing an acceptable standard of residential amenity — so planning may
    properly require a premises to close earlier than its licence permits;
  - **environmental permitting** regulates the operation of the permitted installation, not
    whether the use of the land is acceptable in the first place.

  This answer needs **no evidence about how the other regime has performed**. It is an argument
  about what the regime is *for*, it is consistent with **DM7(1)** (matters controlled by other
  regimes remain material where they have land-use implications) and **DM7(2)** (which bars only
  *duplication*), and decision-makers accept it readily. **Run it whenever the point is raised
  against you.** Where a consultee has given a no-objection, this is also the answer to that —
  say which test they appear to have applied.

  **(b) The performance answer — the regime has not worked here. Evidence required.** Asserting
  that the assumption in P3(3) is displaced *in this case* means rebutting it, and the standard
  is **clear evidence to the contrary**: a complaint history at the premises or at comparable
  premises operated the same way, an unresolved enforcement record, breaches of existing
  conditions, or a consultee's statement that the controls have not worked. **Without that
  evidence, do not run limb (b)** — and note that not running it costs you nothing, because
  limb (a) is available unconditionally.
- *Ask:* that the land-use acceptability be determined on the planning merits now, rather than
  deferred to a regime that asks a different question. **(A)/(B)**
- *One inference to head off, because applicants run it constantly:* **the absence of formal
  action under another regime is not evidence that there is no problem.** That no statutory
  nuisance has been found, no abatement notice served and no enforcement taken says as much
  about thresholds, resources and complaint behaviour as about noise. The converse is not
  symmetrical: a **complaint record** is admissible and does carry weight. So the absence of
  action proves nothing, while its presence proves something — say so if the point is run.

**D7 — Neighbours compelled to keep their windows shut.** (**Direction A** — the mirror of
**D4**, and it has no entry of its own until here.) The acceptability of the development rests,
explicitly or tacitly, on existing neighbours keeping their windows closed.
- *Tell:* internal levels at the *neighbours'* properties quoted for the closed-window condition
  only; a conclusion that disturbance would be acceptable "with windows closed"; an assessment
  that never considers the warm-evening case at the existing receptors.
- *Why it matters:* the loss of the **option** to open a window is itself a harm to living
  conditions — not a neutral mitigation measure that happens to be free. It bites hardest
  through a coincidence that is not accidental: outdoor areas, beer gardens and terraces are at
  their busiest in exactly the warm weather when neighbours' windows are open and they are
  using their own gardens. An assessment that models the development at full occupancy and the
  receptor with the windows shut has modelled two conditions that do not co-occur.
- *Ask:* the assessment run for the **warm-weather, windows-open** condition at the existing
  receptors, and the loss of the ability to open windows and use gardens addressed as harm in
  its own right. **(A)/(B)**

**D8 — Mitigation that someone else has to maintain.** The performance relied on depends on a
structure being kept in good order for the life of the development — and the person who has to
do it is not the applicant.
- *Tell:* an acoustic fence on or astride the boundary, credited with an insertion loss, where
  the **neighbour** will own or share it; planting relied on for attenuation with no mechanism
  for its retention; a barrier installed by the developer and then left to whoever lives there.
- *Why it matters:* a permission is granted for the life of the development, and mitigation that
  degrades — gaps opening at joints, a panel broken and not replaced, a hedge removed — stops
  delivering the performance the conclusion assumed. Where the burden of maintaining it falls on
  the very people the mitigation protects, they are being made responsible for securing their
  own amenity, which is not a proper allocation and not something a condition on the applicant's
  land can fix.
- *Ask:* identify who owns the mitigation, who must maintain it, and what secures that for the
  life of the development. Where it sits on or depends on third-party land, say so — and ask for
  mitigation within the applicant's control instead. **(B)**

**D9 — The mitigation is itself the harm.** The acoustic answer is a structure sized for
performance rather than for its setting: a tall close-boarded barrier, an acoustic enclosure, a
plant building.
- *Why it matters:* the noise case and the design, landscape or heritage case are usually run by
  different people and assessed in different sections of the officer report, so a barrier that
  solves the acoustics and wrecks the street scene can pass both. It should not: the mitigation
  is part of the development, and if it is unacceptable then the noise solution is unavailable
  and the scheme is unmitigated.
- *Tell:* a barrier height, length or material given in the acoustic report that does not appear
  on the elevations or the landscape drawings; mitigation described in text but never drawn.
- *Ask:* the mitigation shown on the application drawings at its **true scale and extent**, and
  its design, landscape, heritage and amenity effects assessed alongside its acoustic
  performance. **(A)/(B)**

## E. Evidence and credibility

**E1 — Predicted versus actual.** Where the applicant or their consultant relies on the same
method, or on case-study data from comparable operating sites, compare **what was predicted at
those sites during their planning applications** with **what has since been measured or
recorded there**.
- *Method:* obtain the earlier application's assessment from the relevant council's portal
  (the **planning-document-search** skill), find the predicted levels at the stated locations,
  and compare them with any later measurements — including measurements in the applicant's own
  subsequent submissions — at the same locations.
- *Why it matters:* a demonstrated divergence between prediction and outcome, using the
  applicant's own documents at both ends, goes directly to the weight the Council can place on
  the prediction in front of it. It is evidence, not argument, and it is the strongest single
  move available to a lay objector.
- *Care — and this note is deliberately severe, because comparator material is the least
  successful evidence an objector puts in.* Material about other sites and other decisions is
  rejected far more often than it is accepted, usually without reaching its substance. Four
  rules:
  - **Produce the document itself.** A reference, a summary, or an excerpt of someone else's
    assessment will be given little or no weight. If you cannot supply the document, do not run
    the point.
  - **Demonstrate comparability before you draw the comparison**, on the factors at **E7** —
    geometry, screening, establishment, hours, noise climate, date. Do it first, in the
    representation, rather than leaving it to be assumed.
  - **Compare like with like** — the same location, the same metric, the same operating
    condition — and state the source of each figure precisely.
  - **Know which kind of comparison you are making.** E1 is *predicted versus measured at the
    same place*, using the applicant's own documents at both ends. That is a genuinely
    different and much stronger thing than "here is another site" or "here is another decision",
    and it should be presented as such so it is not dismissed with them.

  The comparator evidence that does land is direct, first-hand evidence about an **adjacent,
  operating** installation affecting the **same** receptors — which is testimony about this
  case, not a comparison at all.
- *Ask:* the divergence explained, and the weight to be given to the prediction addressed
  expressly in the officer report. **(A)** where the divergence is demonstrated and material.

**E2 — The applicant's own dataset contains what the text ignores.** Case-study or validation
data presented in support of the application containing measured levels — typically maxima —
that the narrative does not discuss, or that exceed the levels predicted for the proposal.
- *Tell:* an appendix graph or table whose peaks are never mentioned in the conclusions; a
  measurement note attributing a high reading to a cause ("shouting", "children") without
  assessing its effect.
- *Tell — read the contours against the receptor, not against the text.* Where the assessment
  includes a **contour or iso-line plot**, find the receptor on it rather than relying on the
  spot level the narrative reports. The text commonly quotes a level at a single chosen
  reception point while a higher contour crosses windows on the same elevation, or a different
  elevation of the same building. The plot is the applicant's own output and it is checkable
  against the elevations without any acoustics at all.
- *Ask:* every measured level in the applicant's own evidence addressed, and its implication
  for the prediction stated; and the levels at the receptor points the contours actually
  indicate, not only at the chosen reception point. **(B)**

**E3 — Unsupported claims of accepted or validated methodology.** "The method is widely
accepted", "the approach has been validated by site measurements", "the methodology is
industry standard" — with nothing cited.
- *Why it matters:* where no prescribed methodology exists for a source (patron and crowd
  noise is the standing example), the burden is on the applicant to justify the method chosen,
  not on the objector to disprove it.
- *Ask:* the validation evidence produced, or the claim withdrawn. **(B)**

**E4 — Assertions about existing operations contradicted by the record.** Claims that
comparable premises operate without issue, that mitigation works in practice, or that the
receptors are insensitive — where complaint records, enforcement history, consultee responses
or representations on the file say otherwise.
- *Method:* the public record is the source — decision notices, officer reports, consultation
  responses and representations on council portals, and information properly obtainable from
  public authorities. Use the **relevant-appeal-finder** skill (advanced repo) for appeal
  decisions on comparable proposals and methods, and note that appeal decisions are **not
  binding precedent** — they are evidence of how the tests have been applied.
- *Care:* keep third-party personal information to the minimum needed, attribute every quote,
  and argue the point on **this** application's facts. A schedule of other decisions is
  supporting context, not a substitute for critiquing the document in front of you — and a
  representation that reads as a campaign against an operator rather than an assessment of a
  proposal will be discounted.
- *Ask:* the claim tested against the record, and the weight given to it addressed. **(B)**

**E5 — The assessment no longer matches the scheme.** The proposal has been amended — capacity,
layout, hours, plant, barrier — and the noise assessment has not been updated.
- *Why it matters:* an assessment of a superseded scheme is not evidence about the scheme
  before the Council, and where enough variables have changed it cannot be relied on at all.
- *Ask:* an updated assessment of the amended proposal, and re-consultation with environmental
  health, before determination. **(B)**

**E6 — The noise assessment contradicts another document in the same submission.** The acoustic
report's assumed facts are checked against the applicant's *other* documents and do not match.
- *Where the contradictions sit,* in rough order of how often they are found:
  - **deliveries and servicing** — the frequency, timing or vehicle type assumed in the
    acoustic report against what the **transport statement** or **servicing management plan**
    says;
  - **servicing location** — the acoustic report assuming a frontage delivery point, while the
    **drawings** show cellar drops, kitchen doors or bin stores on an elevation facing the
    receptor;
  - **access and occupancy** — an outdoor area the planning statement says patrons will not
    use, appearing as an occupied source, or in an appendix figure, in the acoustic report
    itself;
  - **hours** — the operating hours modelled against those on the **application form** or in
    the description (this overlaps **C4**, which is about the gap between the model and what
    the permission would allow; here it is a gap between two of the applicant's own documents);
  - **capacity** — covers, seats or patron numbers in the acoustic report against the
    **layout drawings**, the planning statement or the licensing application;
  - **mitigation that is in the report and not on the drawings** — a barrier, screen, enclosure
    or glazing specification the assessment's conclusion depends on, which appears nowhere in
    the submitted plans, the description or the draft conditions. This is the most consequential
    version of the contradiction, because the assessment is then describing a different
    development from the one the authority would be permitting (run it with **D2** and **D9**);
  - **windows open or shut** — the **overheating or thermal comfort assessment** achieving its
    result by assuming windows open, while the acoustic report requires them closed. See **D4**,
    where this is set out in full; it is the commonest instance of E6 in residential schemes.
- *Why it matters:* a prediction is a function of its assumed facts, and where two documents in
  the same submission assume different facts, at least one assessment rests on a false premise
  — and nothing on the file says which. This is not a drafting quibble: it goes to whether the
  Council has before it a coherent description of the development it is being asked to permit.
  It is also **the single most accessible high-value check in this catalogue**: it requires no
  acoustics, no measurements and no expertise, only the patience to read two documents against
  each other.
- *Method:* build a short table — the assumption, what the acoustic report says, what the other
  document says, and the precise reference for each (document, date, paragraph or drawing
  number). Quote both sides. Do not characterise the discrepancy as deliberate; state it and
  let it speak.
- *Ask:* the contradiction resolved on the file, and the noise assessment re-run on the facts
  actually applied for, **before determination**. **(B)**

**E7 — The comparator premises argument, and how to defeat it.** The applicant points to
somewhere else: another of its own venues, a similar use down the road, a scheme permitted
elsewhere, a noise assessment for a comparable development. "It works there, so it will work
here."
- *Why you need this entry:* the argument is run constantly and **it loses almost every time**
  — but it loses on a specific and reusable ground, and an objector who knows the ground can
  dispose of it in a paragraph instead of arguing about the other site's merits.
- *The test is demonstrated comparability of context, and the burden is on whoever advances the
  comparator.* Ask, for each one:
  - **Geometry** — separation distance, orientation, and what floor the receptors are on. A
    comparator whose nearest receptors are at first-floor level and set back says nothing about
    a ground-floor window three metres away.
  - **Screening and enclosure** — what stands between source and receptor there, and here.
  - **Establishment** — is the comparator a long-established use the neighbours moved to, or a
    recent intensification like this one?
  - **Operating pattern and hours** — and whether the comparator is subject to conditions that
    this proposal would not carry.
  - **The comparator's own noise climate** — usually unstated, and usually the answer.
  - **Date and circumstances** — data gathered years ago, or during an atypical period, is not
    evidence about now.
- *And the standing rebuttal:* consistency in decision-making matters, but **each case is
  decided on its own merits**, and what is acceptable at one site does not translate to another.
  Where the applicant relies on a decision rather than a site, note that a decision produced
  without its full facts is routinely given little weight.
- *Care:* this cuts both ways. An objector's own comparators face exactly the same test — see
  the hardened care note at **E1**.
- *Ask:* for each comparator relied on, the geometry, screening, establishment date, hours and
  noise climate demonstrated to be equivalent; absent that, the comparison to be given no
  weight. **(B)**

**E8 — "There have been no complaints."** Offered as evidence that the use is acceptable, or
that the receptors are not sensitive.
- *The answer, in three parts:*
  - **An absence of complaints is not an absence of harm.** Noise response is subjective and
    complaint behaviour is not a measure of effect: people do not complain because they do not
    know how, expect nothing to change, fear friction with a neighbour, or have simply adapted
    their own behaviour instead — closing windows, not using the garden. The adaptation *is* the
    harm.
  - **People do not complain about what is already permitted.** Where a use operates lawfully
    within its hours, neighbours commonly accept it as settled and do not complain — which tells
    you nothing about how they would experience an **extension**. Say this explicitly where the
    application is to extend; it converts the applicant's best point into a neutral one.
  - **It does not displace the decision-maker's duty.** That a particular neighbour has not
    objected does not remove the obligation to consider the effect on their living conditions.
- *The same reasoning answers "the occupier will know what they are buying"* (Direction B) —
  permission runs with the land, the **second** occupier made no such choice, and a poor
  standard of amenity is not cured by someone having consented to it. Nor can a condition
  restrict occupation to the applicant's relative, or to a person who says they do not mind.
- *Care:* do not overstate this into a claim that complaints *were* made. If there is a
  complaint record, produce it (**E4**); if there is not, argue the absence is uninformative —
  which is a different and perfectly good point.
- *Ask:* where the applicant relies on an absence of complaints, the reason for that absence put
  on the file, and the point given no weight without it. **(B)**

## F. Agent of change — Direction B only

*Use this section only where the application is for **new noise-sensitive development** near an
existing activity. Do not cite P4 against a new noise-generating use.*

> **Two different things travel under the name "agent of change". Keep them apart.**
>
> - **P4 protection of the existing activity — Direction B only.** This is what section F is
>   about: the policy shields an established business, venue or facility from having
>   unreasonable restrictions placed on it by development approved later. Citing it against a
>   new noise-*generating* use argues for the applicant, because the applicant is then the
>   newcomer the policy protects. Expect the point to be taken against you, and expect a
>   decision-maker to disapply P4 expressly where the "existing" activity in fact post-dates
>   the neighbours complaining about it.
> - **The PPG's "onus on the agent of change" — available in *both* directions.** The PPG's
>   noise guidance places the onus on whoever *introduces the change* to identify the effects
>   its operation may cause and the mitigation needed. That is a statement about **where the
>   burden of proof sits**, not about protecting an incumbent, and the agent of change is
>   whichever party is bringing the change — so in Direction A it is the operator seeking the
>   new or extended use. Used that way it reinforces the burden anchor at the top of this
>   catalogue and is a strong Direction A point.
>
> So: **do not cite P4 in Direction A; you may cite the PPG onus in either.** Say which you are
> relying on, because conflating them is what makes an objection look like it has misread the
> policy.
>
> **And check the local policy separately, because it may not follow the national rule.** Many
> development plans carry their own agent-of-change policy, and some are drafted — or are being
> applied — so as to reach **new noise-generating** development as well as new noise-sensitive
> development. Decision-makers differ on this: the same policy has been held engaged against a
> noise generator by one inspector and expressly not engaged by another, on the ground that it
> addresses existing noise-generating activities. So: **run the direction check against the
> words of the policy actually being cited, not against the label "agent of change".** Where a
> local policy plainly does reach your case, cite that policy rather than P4 — and expect the
> point to be contested.

**F1 — The existing activity's operation not identified.** No description of what the existing
business, venue, facility or infrastructure actually does, when, and at what levels.
- *Why it matters:* **P4(3)** requires that "both lawful current **and permitted** levels of
  operation of existing activities" be taken into account — so an assessment based on what the
  operator happens to do now, rather than on what it is entitled to do, understates the
  exposure.
- *Ask:* the existing activity's lawful and permitted operating levels identified and assessed.
  **(B)**

**F2 — No engagement with the existing operator.** No evidence of the early discussions
**P4(2)(a)** contemplates to identify the impacts and inform the scope for mitigation.
- *Ask:* engagement undertaken and its outcome reported before determination. **(B)**
- **⚠️ Do not lead with this one.** The absence of engagement is a real and very common gap in
  applicants' evidence, but it is not what decisions turn on — it is rarely if ever decisive by
  itself, and an objection that opens on procedural engagement looks thin. Run it as support for
  **F1** and **F3**, which is where the substance is. The exception worth having: where
  mitigation depends on the **existing operator changing how they work**, the absence of any
  evidence they are willing to, and of any mechanism to make them, is decisive — but that is
  **F4**, not F2.

**F3 — Mitigation not demonstrated to be deliverable before first occupation.** **P4(2)(b)**
requires the applicant to demonstrate that suitable mitigation **can be provided before first
occupation** if the proposal is to be acceptable.
- *Ask:* deliverability demonstrated, and the mitigation secured by condition or obligation
  with a pre-occupation trigger — which **P4(2)(b)** expressly contemplates. **(B)/(C)**

**F4 — The scheme would restrict the existing use.** The new development's presence would in
practice force the existing activity to curtail hours, reduce activity, or invest in mitigation
of its own — the outcome **P4(1)** is there to prevent.
- *Tell:* an assessment that achieves acceptable internal levels only by assuming the existing
  activity operates below its permitted level, or that proposes conditions on someone else's
  land.
- *Ask:* refusal, or a scheme (layout, orientation, buffering, glazing and ventilation) that
  integrates with the existing activity at its **permitted** level of operation. **(A)**

## G. Procedure and conditions

**G1 — Environmental health not consulted, or their concerns unresolved.** No consultation
response on the file; or a response raising concerns that the applicant has not answered and
the officer report treats as resolved.
- *Ask:* determination deferred until environmental health has responded on the current
  scheme, and any outstanding concerns resolved on the evidence rather than by assertion.
  **(B)**

**G2 — Assessment not by a competent person, or with no stated methodology.** No author,
qualifications, methodology statement, instrumentation details or calibration record.
- *Ask:* the assessment, its author's competence and its methodology stated, with calibration
  and instrumentation records. **(B)**
- **⚠️ Two attacks that reliably fail. Do not make them.**
  - **Generic challenges to the consultant.** Where the report names a recognised consultancy
    and describes its process and equipment, an objection that the author is unqualified or the
    methodology unsound *in the abstract* will be rejected, and it will cost you the credibility
    you need for the specific points. G2 is for a report with **no** stated author, method,
    instrumentation or calibration — not for one you disagree with.
  - **Attacking a revision history.** That figures changed between an earlier and a later
    version of the report, or that an arithmetic error was found and corrected, is **not** a
    deficiency. Reports are revised; corrections are what competence looks like. Fault the
    **final** method or say nothing. (A superseded report the applicant is still *relying* on is
    a different matter — that is **E5**.)

**G3 — Proposed conditions imprecise or unenforceable.** "Noise shall not cause disturbance to
neighbours"; "the management plan shall be adhered to"; a limit with no measurement location,
metric, period or reference condition.
- *Why it matters:* **DM6(1)(c)** requires conditions to be "sufficiently precise to make them
  capable of being complied with and enforced". An unenforceable condition is not mitigation —
  and where the acceptability of the scheme depends on it, the scheme is not acceptable.
- *Ask:* conditions expressed with a **metric, a limit, a measurement location, a time period
  and a reference condition**; hours specified by area and day; capacity fixed by numbers and
  approved layout; plant limits set relative to background at a stated position; verification
  testing after installation and before first use. **(C)**

**G4 — Over-asking.** *(A check on your own draft, not on theirs.)* Requesting refusal where a
precise condition would resolve the point; treating every gap as decision-critical; running
points that are not material (the character of the clientele, competition, property values).
- *Why it matters:* the representation's force comes from being right about the decisive
  points. Padding dilutes them and invites the officer to dismiss the whole. The pattern in
  decided cases is consistent: **the nearest one or two receptors win cases, and area-wide or
  unevidenced limbs are struck out one by one**, costing nothing but credibility. Expect each
  weak limb to be dealt with individually and expressly.
- *A bounded qualification on anti-social behaviour.* Objections to the *type of person* a use
  attracts are not material and will discredit the rest — that rule stands. But ASB is not
  automatically off-limits, and the distinction is worth getting right:
  - **Not material:** the clientele, their character, their conduct as people.
  - **Material:** that the permission would **lengthen the window** in which an existing,
    documented problem occurs. ASB that cannot be attributed to the premises, and could not be
    controlled by condition, can still be relevant on the footing that extending hours gives it
    more time to happen — particularly where the evidence comes from the **police or a council
    community-safety team** rather than from neighbours' characterisations.
  - Run it only on that framing, only on official or documented evidence, and never as an
    argument about who the customers are.
- *One place the burden runs the other way.* Where an **authority** asserts a positive need for
  a restriction going beyond the reason it was originally imposed — a noise condition defended
  on the wider character of the area when it was imposed to protect identified receptors — the
  authority must evidence *that* case, and will lose the limb if it does not. If you are
  supporting a condition, support it on the ground it was actually imposed.
- *Ask:* cut it.

**G5 — "Condition it later": the assessment deferred past the grant of permission.** The
applicant, or the authority, proposes a condition requiring the noise assessment — or the
mitigation scheme, or a survey — to be submitted and approved **after** permission. This is the
single most common way a noise deficiency is made to disappear, and it is refused far more often
than it succeeds.
- *Tell:* a recommended condition beginning "prior to commencement" or "prior to first
  occupation", requiring a noise assessment, scheme of attenuation, or noise management plan to
  be submitted and approved, where **no assessment is before the authority now**. Also: an
  officer report that resolves a consultee's outstanding concern by listing such a condition.
- *Why it matters — and note that this is a **sequencing** objection, not the precision
  objection in **G3**.* The condition may be perfectly precise and still be unlawful here. The
  reasoning is:
  - **The authority cannot know the condition is capable of being complied with.** The deferred
    assessment might show a level that cannot be mitigated at all, or that requires works the
    building cannot accommodate, or that are not permitted development and so cannot be carried
    out. Permission would by then have been granted. A condition whose discharge may prove
    impossible is not "reasonable in all other respects" and fails **DM6(1)**.
  - **Where noise is fundamental to the main issue, the judgement cannot be delegated.** If the
    acceptability of the development *depends* on the answer, the answer has to be before the
    decision-maker when the decision is made. It is not a detail; it is the issue.
  - **It reverses the burden.** The applicant must demonstrate acceptability. A deferred
    condition converts an undischarged burden into a permission.
- *The distinction that keeps this point honest.* Deferral **is** appropriate where the
  performance standard is well understood and the only open question is workmanship — internal
  sound insulation between floors to a stated standard is the standing example, and conditions
  of that kind are routinely accepted. The objection bites where the deferred document would
  determine **whether the development is acceptable at all**, not how it is built. Say which of
  the two you say this is, and do not run the point against a genuine detail condition.
- *Ask:* the assessment provided and consulted on **before determination**, with an express
  submission that a deferred-assessment condition would not meet the condition tests because
  the authority cannot know it could be discharged. **(B)**

**G6 — The trial period or temporary permission.** Offered as a compromise: permit it for
twelve months and see. Attractive to a committee, and it fails on three independent grounds
worth knowing before you either propose or oppose one.
- **It was not consulted on.** Where a temporary period appears for the first time at committee
  or appeal, interested parties have had no opportunity to comment on it, and the harm during
  the trial would be real and would endure.
- **No success criterion, no duration, no method.** A trial that does not state how long it
  runs, what will be measured, where, by whom, against what pre-existing baseline, and what
  happens if the result is adverse, is not a condition capable of being enforced (**DM6(1)(c)**)
  — and a baseline that was never established before the trial began cannot be reconstructed
  afterwards.
- **Harm during the trial is still harm.** Where the objection is that the effect is
  unacceptable, exposing residents to it for a year to find out is not a proportionate answer,
  and is refused as such.
- *Ask:* if a trial is genuinely appropriate, the duration, monitoring method, metric, locations,
  pre-existing baseline and the consequence of an adverse result all fixed in the condition
  before permission. Otherwise, oppose it. **(C)**, escalating where the harm is demonstrated.
