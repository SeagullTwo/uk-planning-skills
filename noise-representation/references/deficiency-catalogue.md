# Noise submission — deficiency catalogue

The recurring, defensible grounds on which a planning application's **noise evidence** (a
Noise Impact Assessment, acoustic report, BS 4142 assessment, plant schedule, or noise
management plan) can be challenged. Each entry is a *pattern to look for*, not a template
sentence: find the specific instance, quote it, and attach the request. Cross-references
point to [`national-guidance.md`](national-guidance.md).

> **Integrity rule (read first).** Only raise a deficiency that is actually present and
> material. If the survey is at the right receptor over a representative period, the right
> standard is applied in full, maximum levels and character are addressed, the assumptions
> are justified, and the mitigation is specified and conditionable, there is **no sustainable
> objection** — say so.
>
> **Three anchors for noise objections:**
> - **The environmental health officer is the ally.** Environmental health is the internal
>   consultee whose remit this is. Read their response first. Where they have made a point,
>   evidence and reinforce it; where they have not yet responded, ask that the application not
>   be determined until they have.
> - **Attack the method, not the decibel.** An objector has no measurements. The winnable
>   ground is that the assessment does not answer the question **P3** asks.
> - **Direction discipline.** Sections A–E and G apply in both directions. Section F is
>   **Direction B only** (new noise-sensitive development near an existing activity) — citing
>   P4 in a Direction A objection argues against your own case.

Sections: **A** scope and baseline · **B** method and metrics · **C** operation and worst case
· **D** mitigation and control · **E** evidence and credibility · **F** agent of change
(Direction B) · **G** procedure and conditions.

## A. Scope and baseline

**A1 — No noise assessment where one is needed.** A use, plant or hours change with an
obvious noise pathway to a nearby sensitive receptor, and nothing in the submission assesses
it.
- *Why it matters:* **Annex C** to the Framework lists, against **P3**, "such information as
  required to demonstrate compliance with relevant aspects of Policy P3". Without it the
  Council cannot make the P3 judgement at all.
- *Ask:* a noise assessment by a competent person, to a stated methodology, **before
  determination**. **(B)**

**A2 — Baseline measured in the wrong place.** The sound level meter sited where it suits the
result rather than where the receptor is — at the kerb of a busy road, on the far side of a
building, at ground level for an upper-floor bedroom.
- *Tell:* the survey location plan does not coincide with the receptor the assessment then
  reports levels *at*; or a measured LAeq dominated by a source (passing traffic) that does
  not reach the receptor in the same way.
- *Why it matters:* a baseline taken in a louder place **overstates** the existing
  environment and therefore **understates** the change the development would cause — the
  single most consequential error in the whole document, because every later conclusion rests
  on it.
- *Ask:* baseline measurements at, or properly corrected to, the actual receptor façades and
  amenity areas, with the location plan and justification. **(B)**

**A3 — Baseline includes the very activity being assessed.** The "existing" survey is
carried out while the use, the outdoor area or the plant is operating — including where it
operates under a temporary permission, a lapsed concession or without permission at all.
- *Why it matters:* it builds the impact into the baseline and then reports little or no
  change. Where the activity is unlawful or time-limited, the correct baseline is the lawful
  fallback position.
- *Ask:* a baseline representing the **lawful** existing situation, with the assessed activity
  excluded. **(B)**

**A4 — Survey too short, or over an unrepresentative period.** One day; weekends only (or
weekdays only); a single season; a period affected by atypical conditions (roadworks, a
closure, unusual weather, an emergency restriction, school holidays).
- *Tell:* the appendix shows a monitoring window of hours rather than days, or dates that
  coincide with something that makes them atypical; no justification of representativeness.
- *Why it matters:* the quietest periods — when the development's noise will be most
  noticeable — are precisely the ones a short or badly-timed survey misses.
- *Ask:* survey data covering the periods when the development would operate *and* the
  quietest periods a receptor experiences, with representativeness justified. **(B)**

**A5 — The statistics show a variable environment, and the assessment ignores it.** Ambient
levels far above background (a large LAeq−L90 gap), or a daytime L10 well above the LAeq, are
the signature of an environment dominated by short loud events with quiet periods between.
- *Why it matters:* in such an environment an average is a poor description of what a
  resident experiences. A development whose noise sits below the *average* can still be
  plainly audible, and intrusive, in the quiet periods between events.
- *Ask:* assessment against the quieter periods the receptor actually experiences, not the
  averaged ambient. **(B)**

**A6 — No attended measurements, no observations, no raw data.** Unattended logging only, with
no record of what was happening, how many people were present, or what the meter was
capturing; raw data and statistical output not provided for scrutiny.
- *Why it matters:* without observations nobody can say whether the measurement represents a
  typical condition, a worst case, or an empty site — so it cannot validate anything. And a
  conclusion whose underlying data is withheld cannot be checked by the Council or its
  consultee.
- *Ask:* attended measurements with observation logs and occupancy records, and the raw
  time-history data and statistical analysis provided. **(B)**

**A7 — Wrong or incomplete receptors.** The assessed "nearest sensitive receptor" is not the
nearest or the most exposed; upper-floor windows, rear elevations, gardens, balconies and
non-residential sensitive receptors (schools, nurseries, care homes, hospitals, places of
worship) omitted.
- *Tell:* a receptor plan that skips the property closest to the source, or reports a façade
  that faces away from it; ground-floor only.
- *Why it matters:* P3 asks about effects on living conditions at the receptors that exist —
  and a mis-selected receptor can understate exposure by a wide margin. Gardens and balconies
  matter in their own right: the PPG treats outdoor amenity space as part of the amenity to
  be protected.
- *Ask:* assessment at the nearest and most exposed receptors, including upper floors and
  external amenity areas, with the selection justified. **(B)**

**A8 — The noise-generating element is characterised as ancillary.** The application presents
the loud component as subordinate to a quieter primary use — a bar "ancillary" to a hotel, a
function room "ancillary" to a restaurant, a taproom "ancillary" to a brewery — and the
assessment then scopes its source terms to the primary use.
- *Tell:* the description and the planning statement emphasise the primary use, while the
  drawings show the "ancillary" element with substantial seating, its own frontage, its own
  entrance, or its own hours; the assessment's source terms account for the primary use only.
- *Why it matters:* **scope is decided before anything is measured**, so a mischaracterisation
  here removes the principal source from the assessment without anyone having to argue about
  decibels. **P3(1)** directs the decision-maker to the likely effects of the development, not
  to the label the applicant has given it. And a use that is genuinely ancillary today can
  intensify to the point of becoming a primary use without a further application, unless the
  permission controls it — so "ancillary" is a description that has to be *secured*, not
  accepted.
- *Ask:* the element assessed at the capacity the drawings and the application form actually
  show; and, where ancillary status is genuinely relied on, that status fixed by condition
  (floor area, covers, hours, independent access). **(B)**, with **(C)** on the securing point.

## B. Method and metrics

**B1 — Averages only: no maximum levels.** The assessment reports LAeq and stops. No LAmax,
no analysis of the loudest events, no commentary on their source.
- *Tell:* tables of LAeq with no LAmax column; or LAmax values present in an appendix or a
  case-study dataset and never discussed in the text.
- *Why it matters:* for intermittent sources — voices, shouting, cheering, glass, doors,
  vehicle movements, plant cycling, deliveries — the discrete loud event is what wakes people
  and what generates complaints. An assessment silent on maximum levels has not assessed the
  effect that actually occurs. This is the single most common finding environmental health
  officers make on assessments of this type.
- *Ask:* maximum levels predicted and assessed at the receptors, for the periods when the use
  would operate, including after dark. **(B)**

**B2 — An averaging period long enough to hide the event.** A 16-hour daytime average applied
to a use that operates for a few hours in the evening; an hourly LAeq covering a period
containing a short intense episode.
- *Why it matters:* averaging a concentrated evening activity across the whole day dilutes it
  to a number that bears no relation to what a resident hears at 9pm. The longer the
  averaging period relative to the activity, the more the assessment understates it.
- *Ask:* assessment over periods matched to the hours the activity would actually operate,
  including a separate evening and night-time assessment where the use extends into them.
  **(B)**

**B3 — Guidance for steady, continuous sources applied to intermittent or human-source noise.**
Criteria derived for road, rail, aircraft or plant noise — anonymous, steady, predictable —
applied without adjustment to voices, crowds, amplified sound, impacts or other variable
sources.
- *Tell:* a WHO community-noise annoyance figure, or a transport-derived criterion, adopted as
  *the* acceptability threshold for a fundamentally different kind of noise, with no
  discussion of its applicability.
- *Why it matters:* the guidance itself is explicit that care is needed where noise has a
  particular character, and the PPG lists the character and the "distinguishability" of the
  source among the factors that influence whether noise is a concern. Human voice is the
  paradigm case: it is intelligible, attention-getting, unpredictable in level, and cannot be
  tuned out the way a constant background can.
- *Ask:* the criterion justified as appropriate for **this** source, or an appropriate
  assessment method adopted. **(B)**

**B4 — Cherry-picked methodology.** The report names a recognised methodology, takes one
number or one step from it, and ignores the rest of what that methodology requires.
- *Tell:* "assessed in accordance with [methodology]" followed by a single change-in-level
  figure, where the same methodology also requires consideration of character, maximum and
  underlying levels, context, the nature of the change and the sensitivity of the receptor.
- *Why it matters:* a methodology's numeric criteria are not severable from its qualitative
  requirements. Selective compliance produces a conclusion the methodology itself does not
  support — and stating a methodology and then not following it is a defect the Council can
  point to on the face of the document.
- *Ask:* the stated methodology applied **in full**, or a different one adopted and justified.
  **(B)**

**B5 — The applicable standard not applied at all.** Commercial or industrial sound (plant,
extraction, refrigeration, servicing, some entertainment sources) assessed without reference
to **BS 4142**; residential internal and external targets addressed without **BS 8233**; a
noise-sensitive residential scheme assessed without **ProPG** or the **AVO Guide**.
- *Ask:* assessment against the applicable standard, or a reasoned justification for the
  method used. **(B)**

**B6 — BS 4142 applied incorrectly.** Where it is used: the background (LA90) determined over
the wrong period or in the wrong place; **no acoustic feature correction** for tonality,
impulsivity, intermittency or other distinguishing characteristics; the context section
skipped; **uncertainty** not quantified.
- *Tell:* a "rating level" identical to the specific sound level (i.e. a +0 dB correction)
  for a source with obvious character; a margin of exceedance smaller than the assessment's
  own stated uncertainty.
- *Why it matters:* the corrections and the context discussion are where BS 4142 does its
  work. Omitting them turns a rating assessment into a bare arithmetic comparison, and can
  move the answer by several decibels — often more than the whole margin the conclusion rests
  on.
- *Ask:* a compliant BS 4142 assessment with feature corrections, context and uncertainty
  addressed. **(B)**

**B7 — The character of the noise never assessed.** Nothing on tonality, impulsivity,
intermittency, distinguishability, intelligibility, low-frequency content, or how the noise
will be *experienced* as against merely measured.
- *Why it matters:* both national guidance and the standards treat character as integral, not
  optional — and the courts have recognised in the statutory-nuisance context that the
  character of noise matters, not merely its absolute level (see `national-guidance.md` on
  *Roper v Tussauds Theme Parks*, persuasive on the point though not a planning authority).
  Two sources at the same measured level can have entirely different effects.
- *Ask:* the character of the noise assessed, and its effect on the receptors addressed
  qualitatively as well as numerically. **(B)**

**B8 — Modelling assumptions unstated, implausible, or unvalidated.** Source terms not given;
a crowd modelled as a single point source or from a single-speaker sound power level; source
heights, directivity, ground and screening effects unstated; the model never validated against
measurement.
- *Tell:* contour plots with no accompanying table of inputs; a stated sound power level with
  no derivation; "the method has been validated" with nothing cited.
- *Why it matters:* a prediction is only as good as its inputs, and inputs that are not
  disclosed cannot be checked. A dispersed crowd, whose individual voices are separately
  audible and intelligible, does not behave like one point source.
- *Ask:* full model inputs, assumptions and derivations disclosed, and validation evidence
  provided. **(B)**

**B9 — Uncertainty ignored, or stated and then disregarded.** No uncertainty analysis; or an
uncertainty of ±3–4 dB stated in the method section, and a conclusion that rests on a
predicted margin smaller than it.
- *Why it matters:* where the stated uncertainty exceeds the margin, the assessment's own
  figures do not support its conclusion. This is a point that can be made from the document
  alone, without any competing measurement — which makes it one of the strongest available to
  a lay objector.
- *Ask:* the conclusion re-stated against the range the uncertainty permits, and the worst
  case within that range assessed. **(B)** — and where the worst case is clearly unacceptable,
  **(A)**.

**B10 — No LOAEL or SOAEL identified.** The assessment never states, for this source and this
context, the level at which adverse effects begin (LOAEL) or become significant (SOAEL) — it
simply compares a predicted level to a borrowed number.
- *Why it matters:* **P3(2)(d)** requires that development "not result in levels of noise
  exposure which would have a **significant observed adverse effect**", and the Framework's
  glossary defines the significant observed adverse effect level. The PPG sets out the
  effect-level hierarchy and what should happen at each. An assessment that does not identify
  where those thresholds lie for this noise cannot demonstrate the policy test is met, and the
  officer report cannot record that it does.
- *Ask:* the LOAEL and SOAEL adopted for this source and context, justified, and the predicted
  exposure assessed against them. **(B)**

**B11 — External levels reported, internal levels never stated.** The assessment predicts
levels at the façade and stops there, so nothing in it says what a resident would hear in the
room — which is the level the residential guideline values are expressed in.
- *Tell:* a table of external levels at the receptor with no corresponding internal figures;
  or internal levels given for the **closed-window** condition only, with no open-window case.
- *Why it matters:* the internal level is what **P3(2)(a)** and the residential guidance are
  about, and the step from one to the other depends entirely on an assumed façade attenuation
  — which in turn depends on whether windows are open or shut. An assessment that leaves the
  step out has not made the comparison the policy requires, and an assessment that makes it
  silently for the closed-window condition has assumed away the ventilation question (**D4**).
- *Care — and this is a hard limit:* **do not perform the conversion yourself.** This skill
  critiques acoustics; it does not produce them, and an objector who supplies their own
  arithmetic invites the whole representation to be dismissed on it. The point is the
  **absence** of the figure and of the stated assumption behind it, argued as such. Where a
  consultee has done the conversion, cite the consultee.
- *Ask:* internal levels stated at the affected rooms for **both** the open-window and
  closed-window conditions, with the assumed façade attenuation stated and justified. **(B)**

**B12 — The authority's own criterion ignored, or borrowed out of its context.** Two opposite
errors, both common, and they are worth checking together because the same local document
usually produces both.
- *Limb 1 — ignored.* The authority publishes its own noise criteria (a plant limit expressed
  as a margin below background, survey requirements, standard condition wordings), and the
  assessment adopts a different and more permissive threshold without mentioning the local
  document at all.
- *Limb 2 — borrowed out of context.* The assessment **does** cite the authority's published
  effect levels, but those figures were derived for a different kind of source — most often
  **transport noise** — and are applied without adjustment to patron, plant or commercial
  noise. Expect the applicant's technical response to argue that the local guidance is not
  expressed as source-specific; the answer is that a criterion derived from transport-noise
  dose-response evidence does not become applicable to human-source noise by being silent
  about its provenance.
- *Why it matters:* the authority's own guidance is the Council's own document, it is usually
  more specific than anything national, and a departure from it is a departure the case officer
  must explain. **PPG 30-007** expressly contemplates noise standards in local policies, and
  **30-006** makes character and distinguishability factors in whether noise is a concern.
- *Ask:* the local criterion applied, or the departure from it justified; and where local
  figures are relied on, their applicability to **this** source demonstrated. **(B)**

## C. Operation, occupancy and worst case

**C1 — Unjustified occupancy and usage assumptions.** A stated proportion of capacity, or of
the time, occupied — with no evidence for it.
- *Tell:* "the area is assumed to be at 50% occupancy for 60% of the operating period" or
  similar, with no survey, no comparator data, no sensitivity test.
- *Why it matters:* occupancy drives the source term directly. An assumption chosen without
  evidence, in the direction that helps the applicant, is not a worst case — and warm evenings
  and weekends, when outdoor areas fill and windows open, are exactly when the assumption
  fails and the impact is greatest.
- *Ask:* the assumption evidenced, and the assessment re-run at **full capacity** in the
  conditions when the area will actually be busiest. **(B)**

**C2 — Capacity not fixed by anything.** The assessment models N patrons, seats or covers, but
nothing in the application would limit the numbers actually present.
- *Why it matters:* a prediction premised on a capacity the permission does not control is
  worthless the day after approval — and outdoor areas are routinely re-laid out to hold more
  than the drawing showed.
- *Ask:* the modelled capacity fixed by condition (numbers and the approved layout), or the
  assessment re-run at the maximum the space can physically hold. **(C)**

**C3 — No worst case.** One scenario modelled — typically a moderate one — with no warm
evening, no weekend or event day, no all-plant-running case, and no sensitivity analysis.
- *Ask:* a reasonable worst-case scenario assessed and reported. **(B)**

**C4 — The modelled operation is not the permitted operation.** The assessment models a
pattern of use narrower than the permission would allow — earlier closing, indoor use only,
doors closed, fewer delivery movements — with no condition tying the permission to it.
- *Why it matters:* permission is granted for what the description and conditions permit, not
  for what the acoustic report assumed. Any gap between them is unassessed impact.
- *Ask:* the permission tied by condition to the modelled operation, or the assessment re-run
  on the operation actually applied for. **(C)**, escalating to **(B)** where the gap is large.

**C5 — Displacement and dispersal not assessed.** When an outdoor area closes, or at closing
time, people move — into another external area, onto the frontage, into the street, to the car
park. Smoking areas, queuing, taxi pick-ups and vehicles starting up are part of the noise the
development generates.
- *Tell:* a curfew on one area presented as mitigation, with no assessment of where the
  occupants go, or of the concentration effect on the area that stays open.
- *Why it matters:* dispersal noise is late, close to bedrooms, and characteristically loud
  and intermittent. A curfew that merely relocates people is not mitigation.
- *Ask:* the effects of displacement, concentration and dispersal assessed at the receptors,
  for the hours applied for. **(B)**

**C6 — Servicing, deliveries, waste and plant left out.** Deliveries, bottle and waste
collection, cellar drops, refrigeration and extraction plant, kitchen ventilation, condensers,
external doors, and their timing — omitted, or covered by a sentence.
- *Tell:* a report about patron noise that never assesses the plant schedule; no assessment of
  early-morning collections; an hours change assessed for patrons only.
- *Why it matters:* these are among the most complained-of sources at operating premises,
  they occur at the most sensitive hours, and they are among the most readily conditioned —
  which makes them worth raising even when the rest of the assessment survives.
- *Ask:* servicing, delivery, waste-collection and plant noise assessed and, where acceptable
  in principle, hours and specifications secured by condition. **(B)/(C)**

**C7 — Cumulative effects not assessed.** The development's noise considered in isolation
from other sources, other consented development, or the other elements of the same scheme.
- *Why it matters:* **P3(1)** expressly requires the likely effects of pollution to be
  considered "including cumulative effects", "whether these effects are on or off-site", and
  "whether this is as a result of the development itself or the product of pre-existing
  conditions in its vicinity".
- *Ask:* a cumulative assessment covering the scheme's own sources together and the other
  relevant sources affecting the same receptors. **(B)**

**C8 — Plant assessed as new, and only as new.** The prediction rests on the manufacturer's
sound power data for equipment in commissioning condition, with no allowance for how it will
perform across the life of the permission and no maintenance regime secured.
- *Tell:* a plant schedule of catalogue figures with no derating, no in-service allowance, and
  no commitment to maintenance; a **BS 4142** assessment finding no acoustic feature to correct
  for, based on equipment that has not yet been installed.
- *Why it matters:* the acoustic features that attract a correction — tonality above all, but
  also impulsivity and intermittency — characteristically *emerge* as equipment wears: bearings,
  fan mountings, anti-vibration mounts and dampers. A permission is granted for the life of the
  development, not for its first month, and an assessment that only ever describes new plant has
  not addressed the condition in which the plant will spend most of its life. The point pairs
  with **D2**: the maintenance regime is exactly the kind of measure that is relied on in the
  conclusion and secured nowhere.
- *Ask:* the reasonable worst case to include an in-service plant condition; and a condition
  requiring the plant to be maintained to the assessed specification, with verification testing
  after installation and a mechanism for re-testing if a tonal or impulsive character develops.
  **(B)** on the assessment, **(C)** on the securing.

## D. Mitigation and control

**D1 — Mitigation assumed to perform perfectly.** An acoustic barrier, fence, canopy or
enclosure credited with a full theoretical insertion loss, with no acoustic specification (mass
per unit area, gaps, joints), no allowance for doors, gates or junctions with existing
structures, and no flanking or reflection analysis.
- *Tell:* a barrier attenuation figure with no specification behind it; a proposed structure
  with no doors shown, where means of escape will require them; no allowance for sound passing
  over, around or through it.
- *Why it matters:* barrier performance is highly sensitive to gaps and to the geometry of
  source and receiver — particularly for receptors at first-floor level and above, where a
  ground-level barrier may provide little benefit at all. Assumed performance that the built
  thing will not deliver is a prediction of a development that will not be built.
- *Ask:* the acoustic specification, the assumptions on gaps, doors and junctions, and
  performance assessed at **upper-floor** receptors as well as ground level. **(B)**

**D2 — Mitigation not secured, or not capable of being secured.** Measures relied on in the
conclusion that appear nowhere in the drawings, the description or the proposed conditions.
- *Ask:* every measure the conclusion relies on secured by condition, with a pre-occupation or
  pre-use trigger and a verification requirement. **(C)**

**D3 — Reliance on operator management measures.** The conclusion rests on staff supervision,
a management plan, signage, "last orders" practice, or a corporate operating policy.
- *Why it matters:* two separate problems, and both should be stated.
  - **Permission runs with the land, not the operator.** A permission granted on the strength
    of one operator's practices survives that operator's departure. Unless a measure is
    captured in a precise, enforceable condition, it does not bind the use.
  - **Supervision is weak control over people noise.** It is a recurring finding of
    environmental health consultees that management measures are of limited effect against
    patron noise, because a small number of people behaving loudly is enough to cause
    disturbance and staff cannot prevent it in real time. Where the applicant relies on such
    measures at other sites, ask what the complaint record at those sites shows.
- *Ask:* the measures the conclusion relies on expressed as **precise, enforceable
  conditions** (**DM6(1)(c)**) — hours, capacity, a boundary noise limit, a closure and
  clearance regime — rather than as management commitments; and, where the effect cannot be
  controlled by condition, that limb of the proposal refused. **(C)**, escalating to **(A)**
  where the only proposed control is management.

**D4 — Mitigation by closed windows, colliding with ventilation and overheating.** (Direction
B.) Internal noise targets met only with windows shut, with no assessment of how the dwellings
are ventilated or kept from overheating.
- *Why it matters:* the PPG is clear that mitigation should not make for an unsatisfactory
  development, and the AVO Guide and ProPG exist precisely because acoustics, ventilation and
  overheating have to be resolved together. **DP3(2)(b)** requires design that minimises
  overheating risk. A scheme that is acceptable only if nobody opens a window is not
  acceptable.
- *Ask:* the acoustic, ventilation and overheating case demonstrated together, with the
  ventilation strategy specified and secured. **(B)**

**D5 — External amenity space of the new development not assessed.** (Direction B.) Internal
levels addressed; gardens, balconies, courtyards, play space and shared amenity areas ignored.
- *Ask:* external amenity areas assessed against the applicable external guideline, and the
  usability of the amenity space demonstrated. **(B)**

**D6 — "Another regime will deal with it."** The assessment, or the applicant's agent, answers
a noise concern by pointing to statutory nuisance powers, environmental permitting or the
licensing regime.
- *Why it matters, and the honest position:* **P3(3)** and **DM7(1)** direct the decision-maker
  to assume, unless there is **clear evidence to the contrary**, that separate regimes will
  operate effectively — so this is not an argument an objector wins by assertion. But the
  assumption is rebuttable, and the land-use question remains the planning system's:
  **DM7(1)** itself says matters controlled by other regimes may be material where they have
  land-use implications, and **DM7(2)** bars only the *duplication* of other regimes'
  controls, not the refusal of an unacceptable use of land.
- *Ask:* where you have it, put the **clear evidence to the contrary** on the file — a
  complaint history at the premises or at comparable premises operated the same way, an
  unresolved enforcement record, breaches of existing conditions, or a consultee's statement
  that the controls have not worked — and ask that the land-use acceptability be determined on
  the planning merits rather than deferred. Without such evidence, do not run this point.
  **(A)/(B)**

## E. Evidence and credibility

**E1 — Predicted versus actual.** Where the applicant or their consultant relies on the same
method, or on case-study data from comparable operating sites, compare **what was predicted at
those sites during their planning applications** with **what has since been measured or
recorded there**.
- *Method:* obtain the earlier application's assessment from the relevant council's portal
  (the **planning-document-search** skill), find the predicted levels at the stated locations,
  and compare them with any later measurements — including measurements in the applicant's own
  subsequent submissions — at the same locations.
- *Why it matters:* a demonstrated divergence between prediction and outcome, using the
  applicant's own documents at both ends, goes directly to the weight the Council can place on
  the prediction in front of it. It is evidence, not argument, and it is the strongest single
  move available to a lay objector.
- *Care:* compare like with like — the same location, the same metric, the same operating
  condition — and state the source of each figure precisely. A mismatched comparison will be
  taken apart and will discredit the rest of the representation.
- *Ask:* the divergence explained, and the weight to be given to the prediction addressed
  expressly in the officer report. **(A)** where the divergence is demonstrated and material.

**E2 — The applicant's own dataset contains what the text ignores.** Case-study or validation
data presented in support of the application containing measured levels — typically maxima —
that the narrative does not discuss, or that exceed the levels predicted for the proposal.
- *Tell:* an appendix graph or table whose peaks are never mentioned in the conclusions; a
  measurement note attributing a high reading to a cause ("shouting", "children") without
  assessing its effect.
- *Tell — read the contours against the receptor, not against the text.* Where the assessment
  includes a **contour or iso-line plot**, find the receptor on it rather than relying on the
  spot level the narrative reports. The text commonly quotes a level at a single chosen
  reception point while a higher contour crosses windows on the same elevation, or a different
  elevation of the same building. The plot is the applicant's own output and it is checkable
  against the elevations without any acoustics at all.
- *Ask:* every measured level in the applicant's own evidence addressed, and its implication
  for the prediction stated; and the levels at the receptor points the contours actually
  indicate, not only at the chosen reception point. **(B)**

**E3 — Unsupported claims of accepted or validated methodology.** "The method is widely
accepted", "the approach has been validated by site measurements", "the methodology is
industry standard" — with nothing cited.
- *Why it matters:* where no prescribed methodology exists for a source (patron and crowd
  noise is the standing example), the burden is on the applicant to justify the method chosen,
  not on the objector to disprove it.
- *Ask:* the validation evidence produced, or the claim withdrawn. **(B)**

**E4 — Assertions about existing operations contradicted by the record.** Claims that
comparable premises operate without issue, that mitigation works in practice, or that the
receptors are insensitive — where complaint records, enforcement history, consultee responses
or representations on the file say otherwise.
- *Method:* the public record is the source — decision notices, officer reports, consultation
  responses and representations on council portals, and information properly obtainable from
  public authorities. Use the **relevant-appeal-finder** skill (advanced repo) for appeal
  decisions on comparable proposals and methods, and note that appeal decisions are **not
  binding precedent** — they are evidence of how the tests have been applied.
- *Care:* keep third-party personal information to the minimum needed, attribute every quote,
  and argue the point on **this** application's facts. A schedule of other decisions is
  supporting context, not a substitute for critiquing the document in front of you — and a
  representation that reads as a campaign against an operator rather than an assessment of a
  proposal will be discounted.
- *Ask:* the claim tested against the record, and the weight given to it addressed. **(B)**

**E5 — The assessment no longer matches the scheme.** The proposal has been amended — capacity,
layout, hours, plant, barrier — and the noise assessment has not been updated.
- *Why it matters:* an assessment of a superseded scheme is not evidence about the scheme
  before the Council, and where enough variables have changed it cannot be relied on at all.
- *Ask:* an updated assessment of the amended proposal, and re-consultation with environmental
  health, before determination. **(B)**

**E6 — The noise assessment contradicts another document in the same submission.** The acoustic
report's assumed facts are checked against the applicant's *other* documents and do not match.
- *Where the contradictions sit,* in rough order of how often they are found:
  - **deliveries and servicing** — the frequency, timing or vehicle type assumed in the
    acoustic report against what the **transport statement** or **servicing management plan**
    says;
  - **servicing location** — the acoustic report assuming a frontage delivery point, while the
    **drawings** show cellar drops, kitchen doors or bin stores on an elevation facing the
    receptor;
  - **access and occupancy** — an outdoor area the planning statement says patrons will not
    use, appearing as an occupied source, or in an appendix figure, in the acoustic report
    itself;
  - **hours** — the operating hours modelled against those on the **application form** or in
    the description (this overlaps **C4**, which is about the gap between the model and what
    the permission would allow; here it is a gap between two of the applicant's own documents);
  - **capacity** — covers, seats or patron numbers in the acoustic report against the
    **layout drawings**, the planning statement or the licensing application.
- *Why it matters:* a prediction is a function of its assumed facts, and where two documents in
  the same submission assume different facts, at least one assessment rests on a false premise
  — and nothing on the file says which. This is not a drafting quibble: it goes to whether the
  Council has before it a coherent description of the development it is being asked to permit.
  It is also **the single most accessible high-value check in this catalogue**: it requires no
  acoustics, no measurements and no expertise, only the patience to read two documents against
  each other.
- *Method:* build a short table — the assumption, what the acoustic report says, what the other
  document says, and the precise reference for each (document, date, paragraph or drawing
  number). Quote both sides. Do not characterise the discrepancy as deliberate; state it and
  let it speak.
- *Ask:* the contradiction resolved on the file, and the noise assessment re-run on the facts
  actually applied for, **before determination**. **(B)**

## F. Agent of change — Direction B only

*Use this section only where the application is for **new noise-sensitive development** near an
existing activity. Do not cite P4 against a new noise-generating use.*

**F1 — The existing activity's operation not identified.** No description of what the existing
business, venue, facility or infrastructure actually does, when, and at what levels.
- *Why it matters:* **P4(3)** requires that "both lawful current **and permitted** levels of
  operation of existing activities" be taken into account — so an assessment based on what the
  operator happens to do now, rather than on what it is entitled to do, understates the
  exposure.
- *Ask:* the existing activity's lawful and permitted operating levels identified and assessed.
  **(B)**

**F2 — No engagement with the existing operator.** No evidence of the early discussions
**P4(2)(a)** contemplates to identify the impacts and inform the scope for mitigation.
- *Ask:* engagement undertaken and its outcome reported before determination. **(B)**

**F3 — Mitigation not demonstrated to be deliverable before first occupation.** **P4(2)(b)**
requires the applicant to demonstrate that suitable mitigation **can be provided before first
occupation** if the proposal is to be acceptable.
- *Ask:* deliverability demonstrated, and the mitigation secured by condition or obligation
  with a pre-occupation trigger — which **P4(2)(b)** expressly contemplates. **(B)/(C)**

**F4 — The scheme would restrict the existing use.** The new development's presence would in
practice force the existing activity to curtail hours, reduce activity, or invest in mitigation
of its own — the outcome **P4(1)** is there to prevent.
- *Tell:* an assessment that achieves acceptable internal levels only by assuming the existing
  activity operates below its permitted level, or that proposes conditions on someone else's
  land.
- *Ask:* refusal, or a scheme (layout, orientation, buffering, glazing and ventilation) that
  integrates with the existing activity at its **permitted** level of operation. **(A)**

## G. Procedure and conditions

**G1 — Environmental health not consulted, or their concerns unresolved.** No consultation
response on the file; or a response raising concerns that the applicant has not answered and
the officer report treats as resolved.
- *Ask:* determination deferred until environmental health has responded on the current
  scheme, and any outstanding concerns resolved on the evidence rather than by assertion.
  **(B)**

**G2 — Assessment not by a competent person, or with no stated methodology.** No author,
qualifications, methodology statement, instrumentation details or calibration record.
- *Ask:* the assessment, its author's competence and its methodology stated, with calibration
  and instrumentation records. **(B)**

**G3 — Proposed conditions imprecise or unenforceable.** "Noise shall not cause disturbance to
neighbours"; "the management plan shall be adhered to"; a limit with no measurement location,
metric, period or reference condition.
- *Why it matters:* **DM6(1)(c)** requires conditions to be "sufficiently precise to make them
  capable of being complied with and enforced". An unenforceable condition is not mitigation —
  and where the acceptability of the scheme depends on it, the scheme is not acceptable.
- *Ask:* conditions expressed with a **metric, a limit, a measurement location, a time period
  and a reference condition**; hours specified by area and day; capacity fixed by numbers and
  approved layout; plant limits set relative to background at a stated position; verification
  testing after installation and before first use. **(C)**

**G4 — Over-asking.** *(A check on your own draft, not on theirs.)* Requesting refusal where a
precise condition would resolve the point; treating every gap as decision-critical; running
points that are not material (the character of the clientele, competition, property values).
- *Why it matters:* the representation's force comes from being right about the decisive
  points. Padding dilutes them and invites the officer to dismiss the whole.
- *Ask:* cut it.
