# Amendment applications — read the parent permission and the whole chain

An amendment application does not stand on its own. What is in front of you is a **delta**:
a covering letter, a few revised drawings, and a description that points at a permission
granted somewhere else on the register. Assessed on its own documents it will nearly always
look modest — because those documents describe what is changing and say almost nothing about
what is being changed.

**The rule this file exists for.** Before assessing any amendment, obtain the **original
permission** — decision notice, full condition set, approved plans, officer/committee report
and any s.106 agreement — **and every amendment already made to it**, then reconstruct what
the permission consists of today. Only then read the current application, and read it as a
delta against that baseline. Assess the change itself *and* the whole run of changes taken
together.

For non-material amendments this is not merely good practice. Section 96A(2) of the Town and
Country Planning Act 1990 requires the authority, in deciding whether a change is material,
to have regard to the effect of the change "together with any previous changes made under
this section, on the planning permission or permission in principle as originally granted".
The PPG puts the same duty on the decision-maker (Paragraph: 008 Reference ID:
17a-008-20140306).

---

## 1. Spotting an amendment application

The tells:

- **The reference suffix** — `NMA` (non-material amendment), `VAR` / `CND` / `DOC`
  (vary or discharge conditions), `MMA` (minor material amendment) and their local variants.
  Suffixes are set by each authority, not nationally — see the reference-number section of
  the **planning-document-search** skill.
- **The description** — "Non-material amendment to [ref]", "Variation of condition N
  (approved plans) of planning permission [ref]", "Removal of condition N of [ref]",
  "Minor material amendment to [ref]".
- **The document set** — thin, and revealing by its thinness: a covering letter, a superseded
  and a proposed drawing, and no fresh technical assessment even where the parent scheme had
  several.
- **A parent reference in the description itself.** It is nearly always there. That is the
  thread to pull.

**Not the same thing:** *amended plans within a live application* — revised drawings
submitted before the application is determined. That is the same application, and the
question there is whether the revision is significant enough to require re-consultation, not
which amendment route applies.

---

## 2. The routes, and what each one opens

### Section 96A — non-material amendment

- **What it does.** The authority "may make a change to any planning permission ... if they
  are satisfied that the change is not material" (s.96A(1)), including imposing new
  conditions or removing or altering existing ones (s.96A(3)) — though the power to remove or
  alter does not extend to the biodiversity gain condition (s.96A(3A)).
- **The test.** There is **no statutory definition of "non-material"** — "it will be
  dependent on the context of the overall scheme; an amendment that is non-material in one
  context may be material in another" (PPG 17a-002-20140306). The authority must be satisfied
  the change is non-material before it can grant.
- **The cumulative duty.** s.96A(2) and PPG 17a-008-20140306 — the effect of *this* change
  **together with previous s.96A changes**, judged against the permission **as originally
  granted**. This is the statutory hook for reading the whole chain.
- **Publicity.** There is **no statutory consultation or publicity requirement**; the
  authority has discretion (PPG 17a-005-20140306). The applicant must notify owners and
  agricultural tenants, who have 14 days to make representations (17a-006-20140306).
- **Determination period** 28 days (17a-007-20140306); **s.38(6) does not apply**, because
  this is not an application for planning permission (17a-008-20140306); there is **no s.78
  right of appeal** (17a-012-20140306).
- **The decision is not a reissue.** "It is not a reissue of the original planning
  permission, which still stands. The two documents should be read together"
  (17a-011-20140306) — the guidance itself instructs the chain read.
- **Not available for listed building consent** (17a-004-20140306).

### Section 73 — application to develop without complying with conditions

- **What it does.** Permission for the development "without complying with conditions subject
  to which a previous planning permission was granted" (s.73(1)) — the route for a **material**
  change, so long as the change is carried by the conditions. "There is no statutory limit on
  the degree of change permissible to conditions under s73, but the change must only relate to
  conditions and not to the operative part of the permission" (PPG 17a-013-20230726).
- **The scope of assessment.** The authority "shall consider only the question of the
  conditions subject to which planning permission should be granted" (s.73(2)). The principle
  of the development is not reopened — but the **effects of the varied condition are fully in
  play**, and so is current policy (see §5).
- **What it cannot do.** It cannot extend the time limit for starting development or for
  applying for approval of reserved matters (s.73(5); PPG 17a-014-20140306), and it **cannot
  be used to change the description of the development** (17a-014-20140306). It does not apply
  at all where the parent permission's time limit has expired without development having begun
  (s.73(4)), and it cannot disapply the biodiversity gain condition (s.73(2B)).
- **It needs a plans condition.** "Section 73 cannot be used if there is no relevant condition
  in the permission listing the originally approved plans" — a s.96A can add one, "which would
  then enable the use of a section 73 application to make minor material amendments"
  (17a-018-20230726). A s.96A followed by a s.73 is therefore a recognised sequence, and a
  reason to look for both.
- **It creates a second permission.** A s.73 grant "takes effect as a new, independent
  permission to carry out the same development as previously permitted subject to new or
  amended conditions. The new permission sits alongside the original permission, which remains
  intact and unamended", and the applicant chooses which to implement (17a-015-20140306). The
  decision notice should restate the earlier conditions that continue to have effect.
- **EIA.** A s.73 application "is considered to be a new application for planning permission
  under the 2017 EIA Regulations" — a fresh screening opinion is required for Schedule 2
  development meeting the criteria or thresholds, and an Environmental Statement must be
  submitted for EIA development whether or not the original one needs updating
  (17a-016-20140306).
- **The s.106 does not follow automatically.** "If the original permission was subject to a
  planning obligation then this may need to be the subject of a deed of variation"
  (17a-015-20140306).

### Section 73B — permission not substantially different from an existing permission

- Inserted into the 1990 Act by s.110(2) of the Levelling-up and Regeneration Act 2023, this
  route would allow variation of **both the description and the conditions**, subject to the
  authority being satisfied that the effect "will not be substantially different from that of
  the existing permission", with consideration limited to the respects in which the new
  permission would differ.
- ⏳ **Not in force at verification** (legislation.gov.uk records s.110 as not in force at
  Royal Assent, s.255(3)(b), with no commencement note; it also needs a development order to
  operate). **Check its status before treating it as available**, and do not accept a
  description citing it without checking.

### Routes that are *not* amendments — don't confuse them

- **Approval of reserved matters** under an outline permission, and **discharge of / approval
  of details reserved by** a condition. These deliver detail the permission always required;
  they do not change it. They are still worth reading, because they fix what the permission
  now consists of.
- **s.73A** — permission for development already carried out (retrospective).
- **A fresh application under s.70.** Where the modifications are "fundamental or substantial",
  a new application is required and none of the amendment routes will do (PPG
  17a-001-20140306). "This should have been a fresh application" is a proper submission.

## 3. Rebuild the chain before assessing anything

1. **Take the parent reference from the description.** If the description does not name one,
   search the address on the portal and reconstruct the history from the register.
2. **Get the parent decision notice in full** — every condition, verbatim, and the
   **approved-plans condition** with its drawing numbers and revision letters.
3. **Get the parent officer or committee report.** This is where the *reasons* live: the
   judgment the permission rests on, and the mitigation the acceptability turned on ("acceptable
   only because …"). An amendment that removes that mitigation is a different application from
   one that does not, and only the report shows which.
4. **Get the s.106 agreement or unilateral undertaking**, and any deed of variation.
5. **List every subsequent application on that permission** — s.96A amendments, s.73
   variations, condition discharges, and any appeal — and read each one's decision notice.
6. **Build the baseline**, in date order: the operative description, the condition set **as
   varied**, the current approved-drawing numbers and revisions, and the obligations as varied.
   Write it down; the whole assessment hangs off it.
7. **Only now read the current application**, as a delta against that baseline.

Where the chain cannot be completed — a document is not on the register, or a reference is
attested but not retrievable — **say so explicitly** and record which link is missing. A
partial baseline stated as a complete one is worse than an acknowledged gap.

## 4. What to compare

Take the delta from the **drawings and the conditions**, not from the covering letter's
account of itself. Compare, item by item:

- the **description of development** — any change to it is outside what s.73 can carry;
- the **approved drawing numbers and revision letters** — the commonest hiding place, because
  a plans-condition variation can substitute a substantially redesigned drawing without a word
  of the description changing;
- **dimensions** — footprint, depth, height to eaves and ridge, plot coverage, finished floor
  and site levels, separation distances;
- **quantum and mix** — unit numbers, bed mix, floorspace, storeys;
- **affordable housing** — amount, tenure, and where it sits in the scheme;
- **access, parking and servicing**;
- **landscaping, tree retention, boundary treatment and screening**;
- **materials and elevational treatment**;
- **ecology and BNG** — habitat retention, the metric, and the delivery mechanism;
- **drainage and SuDS**;
- **conditions removed, relaxed or reworded**, and **obligations varied or released**.

## 5. The three questions to answer

1. **Is the route right?** Is the change genuinely non-material for a s.96A — judged in the
   context of the whole scheme, cumulatively with earlier changes? Does a s.73 keep to the
   conditions and leave the operative description alone? If not, the answer is that the change
   needs a different application, and that is a complete submission in itself.
2. **What does the change itself do?** Assess the delta on its merits against the development
   plan. Note that a s.73 grants a *new* permission determined **now**: the plan, the national
   framework and the evidence base as they stand at the date of this decision apply — not those
   in force when the parent was granted — and fresh duties (BNG, EIA screening) attach to the
   new application.
3. **What has the chain done, taken together?** Compare today's proposal with the permission
   **as originally granted**. This is the statutory question for a s.96A and the honest one for
   any amendment.

### Cumulative drift — the patterns to look for

- **Ratcheting** — height, footprint or depth increased by a small increment at a time, each
  one defensible alone.
- **Thinning mitigation** — landscaping, screen planting, acoustic fencing or boundary
  treatment progressively reduced across successive revisions of the plans condition.
- **Eroding obligations** — affordable housing amount or tenure changed by condition variation
  and deed of variation.
- **Removing the load-bearing condition** — the single measure the original officer report
  relied on to conclude the scheme acceptable.
- **Silent drawing substitution** — new revision letters against an unchanged condition
  number, with no narrative of what changed.

## 6. Grounds available on an amendment — and what is not one

Proper grounds:

- **Wrong route** — the change is material, so s.96A cannot carry it; or it alters the
  operative description, which s.73 cannot do. Ask that it be dealt with as a fresh
  application on which the public can be consulted.
- **Harm from the delta itself**, assessed against named development-plan policies.
- **Loss of the mitigation the permission depended on**, evidenced from the parent officer
  report.
- **Cumulative materiality** — the change together with the earlier changes is material,
  citing s.96A(2).
- **A duty not discharged on the new application** — no EIA screening on a s.73 for Schedule 2
  development, no BNG plan, no updated survey where the original is stale.

Not grounds:

- **Re-arguing the principle.** It was settled by the parent permission and a s.73 reopens only
  the conditions. An objection that reads as opposition to the permitted development, rather
  than to the change, will be set aside.
- **Objecting to what the parent permission already allows** — including elements the amendment
  leaves untouched.

---

## 7. Traps

- **Silence is not acquiescence.** A s.96A carries no statutory publicity, and a s.73 only
  discretionary consultation. Few or no third-party representations tell you nothing about the
  scheme's acceptability, and a neighbour may learn of the change only when it is built.
- **"Minor material amendment" is a practice term, not a statutory one.** The application is a
  s.73; test it against s.73's limits, not against the label.
- **The fallback only counts if it can still be built.** An extant parent permission is a real
  planning fallback, but where development already carried out makes it physically impossible
  to complete the earlier scheme, further development under it is unlawful — the *Pilkington*
  principle, affirmed in *Hillside Parks Ltd v Snowdonia National Park Authority* [2022] UKSC
  30 (2 November 2022). Check whether the fallback being relied on is still available.
- **The obligation does not vary itself.** A new permission under s.73 does not carry the
  parent's s.106 across; look for the deed of variation, and note its absence.
- **Expired permissions.** s.73 is unavailable where the parent's commencement time limit has
  passed without development having begun (s.73(4)).
- **Portals rarely link the chain properly.** Related-application panels are incomplete on many
  systems; search by the parent reference *and* by address, and cross-check against any appeal.
