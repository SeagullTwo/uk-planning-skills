# Materiality — the test, and the standard lists

Reference file for `planning-report-quality`. Read before recording a materiality verdict.

## The test

A consideration is material where it:

- **relates to the development and use of land**;
- serves a **planning purpose**, not an ulterior one; and
- **fairly and reasonably relates** to the development being determined.

The weight to be given to a material consideration is for the decision-maker. Whether
something is material at all is a question of law. Those are different questions and this
skill keeps them apart — materiality in step 2, weight in step 3.

## Ordinarily material

- The development plan and its policies, and the degree of compliance with them.
- National policy and guidance.
- Design, scale, massing, layout, materials, and effect on character and appearance.
- Effect on residential amenity — overlooking, overshadowing, overbearing effect, noise,
  odour, light.
- Highway safety, access, parking and traffic generation.
- Heritage impact, including on setting.
- Ecology, protected species, habitats and biodiversity net gain.
- Flood risk and drainage.
- Contamination and land stability.
- Effect on trees and landscape.
- Planning history of the site, and appeal decisions on it or on comparable sites.
- The provision, or absence, of infrastructure and affordable housing.
- Consistency with decisions on comparable applications.
- Public representations, **to the extent they raise planning matters**.

## Ordinarily not material

These recur in representations and sometimes reach reports. Where one is given weight, that
is a legal error, not a stylistic complaint.

- **Loss of a private view.** There is no right to a view.
- **Effect on property values.**
- **Competition with an existing business**, or loss of trade.
- **The applicant's identity, motives, character or conduct**, including their planning
  history elsewhere.
- **Private covenants, boundary disputes and land ownership**, which are civil matters.
- **Loss of private rights** — of light, of access, of parking on the highway.
- **Moral objections** to a lawful use.
- **The number of objectors**, as opposed to the planning content of what they raise.
- **Matters controlled by another regime** where that regime is the proper vehicle —
  building regulations, licensing, environmental permitting, highways adoption. These are
  not always immaterial, but the report should say why it is engaging with them.
- **Speculation about future applications** not before the authority.

## Material only in a stated circumstance

Record these as *material only if*, with the condition named:

- **Fear of crime or of a use** — material where it is objectively justified and
  land-use-related, not where it rests on assumption about future occupiers.
- **Personal circumstances of an applicant or occupier** — exceptionally material, usually
  where a policy expressly provides for it, and rarely determinative.
- **Prematurity** — material only on the narrow national-policy basis, not as a general
  objection to deciding now.
- **Financial viability** — material where a policy requirement turns on it, and then only
  on assessed evidence.
- **Precedent** — material only where the decision would genuinely make a like decision
  harder to resist, not as a general anxiety.

## Two traps

**An immaterial matter may be mentioned.** A report that records an objection about property
values and then states it is not a material consideration has done the right thing. The
defect is giving it **weight**, not naming it. Check what the report does with it before
recording an error.

**A material matter raised and dismissed is not an omission.** Step 5 looks for
considerations never addressed at all. A consideration addressed and rejected has been taken
into account, and disagreeing with the rejection is a weight finding, not an omission.
