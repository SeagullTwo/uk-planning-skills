# planning-report-quality

**How good is this planning assessment, and how do you know?**

Takes a written planning assessment — an officer report, a delegated report, an appeal
statement, an agent's planning statement — and measures it one assertion at a time.

## Four tests, kept separate

Every claim in the report is tested on four axes, and the axes never merge:

| Test | Asks |
|---|---|
| **Accuracy** | Is the assertion correct on the evidence? |
| **Materiality** | Is it a planning consideration at all? |
| **Weight** | How much is made of it, and is that justified? |
| **Load-bearing** | Does the recommendation depend on it? |

Each maps to a way a planning decision fails in public law — material error of fact, an
immaterial consideration taken into account, a conclusion unsupported by evidence. That is
what makes the output explainable to someone who asks where a number came from.

A fifth check runs separately: **what material consideration was never addressed at all**,
which no claim-by-claim pass can find because nothing in the text points at it.

## Why the axes stay apart

Because collapsing them describes the author unfairly.

A true observation that turns out not to matter is not the same failing as an assertion that
is simply wrong. In the source research, a single combined figure reported 51% for work that
was 80% accurate — the gap was entirely true-but-immaterial observations, counted as errors.
One number could not tell the difference. Four can.

The corollary matters in planning practice: **identifying a true point is never itself a
fault.** Whether it is relevant, and what weight it carries, is a separate judgement made
later and by someone else.

## Output

- A **line-by-line table** — one row per claim, every claim tested, nothing sampled.
- **Counts with stated formulas**, including the denominator and the scope it covers.
- A fixed **"how to read these numbers"** paragraph, so the same question always gets the
  same answer.

The headline is the **load-bearing error count**: assertions that are both wrong and
necessary to the recommendation. A count of specific quotable things, checkable by reading
six rows.

**There is no composite quality score, deliberately.** A composite would recombine what the
four tests exist to separate.

## What it does not do

- It does not say whether the decision was right. A sound report can support a decision you
  disagree with.
- It does not advise on challenge. It locates and characterises; a solicitor advises.
- It does not test judgements for truth. A judgement can be reasoned or unreasoned, never
  correct or incorrect, and demanding proof of one is the most common way a review of a
  report goes wrong.
